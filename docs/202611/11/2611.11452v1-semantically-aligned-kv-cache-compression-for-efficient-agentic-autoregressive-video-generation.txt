ABSTRACT
Agentic autoregressive video generation offers a promising approach to control-
lable video synthesis through semantic planning, sequential generation, and visual
verification. However, growing visual histories enlarge the key–value (KV) cache,
increasing memory use and attention costs across candidate rollouts. KV cache
compression reduces this overhead, but existing methods primarily optimize individ-
ual generators without explicitly accounting for the planner’s semantic constraints.
Applying them directly can assign low importance to states associated with par-
ticular requested attributes or actions, potentially weakening semantic fidelity. In
this paper, we introduce SEMANTICKV, a training-free method for semantically
aligned KV cache compression. To our knowledge, SEMANTICKV is the first
KV cache compression method for agentic autoregressive video generation that
explicitly uses planner-extracted semantic constraints to guide retention. Text
cross-attention identifies constraint-related video positions, whose self-attention
queries are collected into separate groups. Attention weights are averaged within
each group and maximized across groups, giving each cached entry a high score
when it is strongly used by any represented constraint. A single top-k selection
retains high-scoring entries under the memory budget, leaving their keys, values,
and positions unchanged. For fixed query groups and equal entry costs, this se-
lection minimizes an additive upper bound on worst-group discarded attention.
The method operates independently within each candidate without retraining or
additional video generations. Our evaluation spans four AR video backbones and
three benchmarks, covering 5-, 30-, and 60-second generation; our method achieves
strong performance across these settings. On Causal Forcing at a compression
ratio of 0.7, SEMANTICKV obtains a VBench Total Score of 84.10, compared with
84.05 for the strongest compressed baseline and 83.99 for Full KV. Meanwhile, it
achieves a speedup of 1.22× over Full KV and reduces KV-cache memory from
6.04 GB to 1.81 GB, corresponding to a 3.35× reduction.

INTRODUCTION
Agentic autoregressive (AR) video generation offers a promising approach to producing controllable
videos from complex user requests (Zeng et al., 2025; Feng et al., 2026; Huang et al., 2026b). A
semantic planner clarifies the requested content and refines the prompt (Yang et al., 2026d; Cudlenco
et al., 2026; Huang et al., 2025e), an AR generator synthesizes frames or latent chunks sequentially
from previously generated content (Henschel et al., 2025; Gao et al., 2024), and a visual critic assesses
generated candidates against the request (Long et al., 2026). These complementary roles address
the need to both specify intended content and check whether the resulting video follows it. This
1

workflow, however, incurs substantial inference overhead. Each rollout stores historical keys and
values in a KV cache to reuse information from earlier chunks (Xing et al., 2024; Gao et al., 2025).
Longer visual histories increase cache storage and attention computation, while generating multiple
candidates repeats these costs even when their caches are not resident simultaneously.
KV cache compression alleviates this bottleneck by reducing the historical states stored and accessed
during attention. Representative methods include R-KV (Cai et al., 2025), which selects cached
states using importance and redundancy; Dummy Forcing (Guo et al., 2026), which allocates
context according to attention-head locality; and Forcing-KV (Ji et al., 2026), which applies different
retention policies to static and dynamic heads. Other approaches exploit temporal redundancy (Chen
et al., 2026a), future-query statistics (Luo et al., 2026a), historical coverage (Zhao et al., 2026), or
instance-specific context representations (Fu et al., 2026). These methods primarily optimize context
use within an individual generator, rather than explicitly connecting cache retention to the semantic
constraints produced by an agentic planner.
Directly applying such policies within an agentic workflow leaves two closely related limitations.
First, the planner distinguishes user-specified subjects, attributes, relations, and actions, but cache
selection need not identify which states are used by computations associated with each constraint. A
detailed plan can therefore guide prompt construction without guiding the history retained during
generation. Second, when attention observations are pooled into a global importance score, a state
strongly used for one attribute or action can be assigned a low average score because unrelated queries
give it little attention. Discarding such states may weaken the information supporting the requested
content. The resulting deviations can propagate through subsequent chunks (Liu et al., 2026; Guo
et al., 2025) and compromise long-horizon consistency (Xie et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025). The
missing capability is thus not merely stronger compression, but semantically aligned retention that
carries the planner’s explicit constraints into cache selection.
In this paper, we introduce SEMANTICKV, a training-free method for semantically aligned KV cache
compression in agentic autoregressive video generation. To our knowledge, SEMANTICKV is the first
KV cache compression method in this setting to explicitly use planner-extracted semantic constraints
to guide retention. Its central idea is that the semantics that guide video generation should also guide
the history its generator retains. The existing semantic constraints R determine which generator
queries are observed and how their cache-attention weights are grouped. This connects retention to
the requested content while preventing low scores from unrelated groups from diluting an entry’s
importance to a particular constraint. The planner, generator, and visual-verification workflow remain
unchanged.
Specifically, SEMANTICKV first matches each semantic constraint to its words in the candidate
prompt. Text cross-attention identifies video positions that attend strongly to these words, and
their actual self-attention queries form a constraint-specific group. For each KV entry, the method
averages attention weights within each group and takes the maximum across groups to obtain one
semantic importance score. Thus, an entry can receive high priority even when it is primarily
used by only one represented constraint. At each completed-chunk cache update, a single top-k
selection retains the highest-scoring eligible entries within the byte budget, alongside states required
by the backbone. Retained keys, values, and positions are packed unchanged, and each candidate
uses its own observations and cache. Our local analysis relates discarded attention to groupwise
attention-output changes and shows that, for fixed query groups and equal entry costs, top-k selection
minimizes an additive upper bound on worst-group discarded attention (Appendix D). This provides
a theoretical basis for the scoring rule without adding another optimization stage.
To evaluate SEMANTICKV, we conduct experiments on four AR video backbones: LongLive (Yang
et al., 2026c), LongLive 2.0 (Chen et al., 2026b), Self Forcing (Huang et al., 2025d), and Causal Forc-
ing (Zhu et al., 2026). We use three benchmarks—VBench (Huang et al., 2024), VBenchLong (Huang
et al., 2025f), and VisionReward (Xu et al., 2026)—covering short-video quality, long-horizon con-
sistency, and preference-based assessment at 5-, 30-, and 60-second durations. Comparisons include
three KV cache compression baselines and Full KV under matched generation settings and realized
cache budgets. Two targeted ablations isolate constraint-conditioned query selection and groupwise
score aggregation, while efficiency analysis reports cache memory and generator throughput. On
Causal Forcing at a compression ratio of 0.7, SEMANTICKV obtains a VBench Total Score of 84.10,
compared with 84.05 for the strongest compressed baseline and 83.99 for Full KV. Meanwhile, it

RELATED WORK
Agentic autoregressive video generation.
Autoregressive video models generate causally ordered
frames or latent chunks for streaming and long-horizon synthesis (Chen et al., 2024; 2025; ai et al.,
2025; Deng et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2024b), with advances in rollout stability, efficiency, and
memory modeling (Liu et al., 2026; Guo et al., 2025; Lin et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025; Luo et al.,
2026b). Agentic systems add planning, generation, and feedback (Yuan et al., 2024; Liang et al.,
2025; Lin et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2024; Hu et al., 2024) through staged production, role-based
collaboration, and hierarchical planning (Zhuang et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025), as
well as experience- or feedback-driven refinement (Wang et al., 2024a; Soni et al., 2024). Longer
visual histories increase KV-cache costs within each rollout, and candidate generation repeats this
overhead. SEMANTICKV keeps the planning–generation–verification workflow and AR backbone
unchanged, using the planner’s semantic constraints to guide cache retention within each candidate.
KV cache compression.
KV cache compression is a common technique for model compression
and acceleration (Huang et al., 2025c;a;b; Zhang et al., 2026b). KV cache compression reduces
inference costs by selecting or compacting cached keys and values. Approaches include merging
and salience-based retention (Samuel et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026a), compact representations
and structured selection (Xi et al., 2026; Ma et al., 2026; Mao et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026; Luo
et al., 2026a), context management (Li et al., 2026a; Meng et al., 2026; Huang et al., 2026c; Zhang
et al., 2026a), and response-aware compression (Feng et al., 2025b; Gu et al., 2025; Zweiger et al.,
2026; Li et al., 2026c; Yang et al., 2026b). Representative methods include R-KV (Cai et al., 2025),
which selects states by importance and redundancy; Dummy Forcing (Guo et al., 2026), which limits
context by head locality; and Forcing-KV (Ji et al., 2026), which preserves transition anchors for
static heads and prunes similar segments for dynamic heads. These methods do not explicitly use
planner-extracted semantic constraints, potentially overlooking task-relevant states. SEMANTICKV
instead uses these constraints to identify and retain relevant KV states within the memory budget.

by queries associated with the planner’s semantic constraints R. We first identify these queries, then
aggregate their attention into one score per KV entry and apply a single top-k selection under the
memory constraint in Eq. 11.

Returning to autoregressive generation.
If all available entries fit, the cache remains unchanged.
Otherwise, we score once, select once, and pack the retained keys, values, and position records in
their original logical order, breaking score ties by original position index. Retained vectors and
positions remain unchanged, with no rescoring after deletion. The compressed cache generates the
next chunk as in Eq. 10; subsequent updates consider retained history together with new KV states.
Both candidates use the same policy and fixed R, but their own prompts, queries, and caches, without
critic feedback during generation. Query collection, scoring, selection, and packing count toward
runtime, and their temporary buffers count toward peak memory. Figure 2 illustrates the complete

5.1
EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
Models.
We evaluate four autoregressive video diffusion models spanning different model designs
and parameter scales: LongLive (Yang et al., 2026c), LongLive 2.0 (Chen et al., 2026b), Self
Forcing (Huang et al., 2025d), and Causal Forcing (Zhu et al., 2026). The evaluated models cover a
broad range of model sizes, with parameter counts ranging from 1.3B to 5B.
Baselines.
We compare SEMANTICKV with representative KV-cache compression methods that
provide publicly available implementations and can be reliably integrated into autoregressive video
diffusion models. Specifically, we compare with Full KV, R-KV (Cai et al., 2025), Dummy
Forcing (Guo et al., 2026), and Forcing-KV (Ji et al., 2026). For all compressed methods, we follow
their official implementations and apply the same KV-cache compression ratio whenever possible to
ensure a fair comparison. Appendix B.2 provides further details.
Benchmarks.
We evaluate video generation quality using three complementary benchmarks:
VBench (Huang et al., 2024), VBenchLong (Huang et al., 2025f), and VisionReward (Xu et al., 2026).
For short-video evaluation, we generate 5-second videos using all 946 official VBench prompts.
For long-video evaluation, we use 128 prompts from MovieGen (Polyak et al., 2024) to generate
30-second and 60-second videos, with five random seeds for each prompt. We additionally employ
VisionReward on the 100-prompt evaluation set used by Causal Forcing. We evaluate all 16 VBench
dimensions. Detailed metric definitions appear in Appendix B.4.
Implementation Details.
All experiments are run on a server equipped with 8× NVIDIA H20
96GB GPUs. Our implementation uses PyTorch for video generation and serves Qwen3.5-9B through
vLLM for semantic planning and pairwise visual verification. The default launcher assigns seven
GPUs to independent generation workers and one GPU to planning and verification. All four video
backbones use BF16 precision and four denoising steps; model-specific resolutions, frame rates,
and chunk sizes are detailed in Appendix B.1. We evaluate five base seeds, 42–46, generating two
candidates sequentially per run. The critic samples m = 6 frames per candidate, and candidate
selection uses δ = 1.0, τ = 0.55, and utility weights (wv, ws, wt, wm) = (0.4, 0.2, 0.3, 0.1). Both
the planner and critic use temperature zero.

5.3
ABLATION STUDIES
Component ablations.
We ablate constraint-conditioned query selection and groupwise aggregation
under the same cache budget. SEMANTICKV improves the Semantic Score from 76.94 to 77.18
over uniform queries and from 76.78 to 77.18 over pooled constraints, while maintaining similar
throughput. These results support the contributions of both semantic query selection and constraint-
wise aggregation. Complete results are reported in Appendix F.1.
Query limit.
With the cache budget fixed, nq = 32 achieves the highest Total Score (81.68) among
nq ∈{16, 32, 64} while reaching 10.00 FPS. We therefore use nq = 32 as the default setting;
detailed results are provided in Appendix F.2.
5.4
ADDITIONAL ABLATION STUDIES
Paired semantic analysis.
On matched prompt–seed outputs, SEMANTICKV shows positive gains
in 15 of 18 semantic dimension–baseline comparisons. The six-dimension macro improvements over
R-KV, Dummy Forcing, and Forcing-KV are 1.00, 0.86, and 1.25 points, respectively, indicating a
9


consistent advantage in semantic fidelity. Detailed paired results and bootstrap intervals are provided
in Appendix G.1.
Semantic complexity.
We further analyze performance with respect to the number of planner-
extracted semantic constraints. The gains of SEMANTICKV become more pronounced on multi-
constraint prompts, reaching macro improvements of 4.62, 3.26, and 7.24 points over R-KV, Dummy
Forcing, and Forcing-KV for two-constraint prompts. Results for different semantic-complexity
groups are reported in Appendix G.2.
Temporal semantic drift.
We also evaluate within-video semantic drift using CLIP-based prompt
alignment and visual similarity. The compressed methods show only small differences, with all paired
confidence intervals between SEMANTICKV and the compressed baselines including zero. This
suggests that the semantic gains of SEMANTICKV do not introduce detectable additional temporal
drift on this subset. Detailed results are provided in Appendix G.3.
6
CONCLUSION
We introduce SEMANTICKV, a training-free method for semantically aligned KV cache compression
in agentic autoregressive video generation. Its central idea is to use the planner’s semantic constraints
not only to guide generation, but also to guide cache retention. Constraint-conditioned queries provide
groupwise attention scores, and one top-k selection retains high-scoring KV entries under the byte
budget. The method preserves native keys, values, and positions and leaves the outer agentic workflow
unchanged. Its practical benefit is assessed through matched semantic-quality, cache-memory, and
throughput measurements rather than inferred from attention scores alone.

B.1
DETAILS OF MODELS
Basic information.
We evaluate four autoregressive video diffusion models spanning different
model scales and generation designs.
• LongLive (Yang et al., 2026c). LongLive is a 1.3B-parameter autoregressive video diffusion
model for real-time long-video generation. It adopts frame-level causal generation with KV
caching and introduces a frame sink, short attention window, and KV recaching to support
efficient long-horizon and interactive generation.
• LongLive 2.0 (Chen et al., 2026b). LongLive 2.0 is a 5B-parameter autoregressive video
diffusion model designed for scalable long-video generation. It extends the LongLive
framework to a larger backbone and supports long-horizon generation with KV-cached
causal attention and efficient sequence-parallel inference.
• Self Forcing (Huang et al., 2025d). Self Forcing is a 1.3B-parameter autoregressive video
diffusion model based on Wan2.1-T2V-1.3B (Wan et al., 2025). It performs causal video
generation with temporal KV caching and supports both frame-wise and chunk-wise autore-
gressive generation, making it a representative backbone for efficient short-video generation.
• Causal Forcing (Zhu et al., 2026). Causal Forcing is a 1.3B-parameter autoregressive video
diffusion model derived from Wan2.1-T2V-1.3B (Wan et al., 2025). It performs few-step
causal generation with temporal KV caching and provides an additional autoregressive
formulation for evaluating the generalization of our method.
Model-specific inference configurations.
We use the following inference configurations for the
four backbones. Resolutions are reported as width × height, and chunk sizes are measured in latent
17


frames. The video frame rates below describe playback rather than the generation throughput reported
in our efficiency evaluation.
• LongLive (v1). We use BF16 precision, 832 × 480 resolution, a video frame rate of 16 FPS,
four denoising steps, and three latent frames per autoregressive chunk.
• LongLive 2.0. We use BF16 precision, 1280 × 704 resolution, a video frame rate of 24 FPS,
four denoising steps, and eight latent frames per autoregressive chunk.
• Self Forcing. We use BF16 precision, 832 × 480 resolution, a video frame rate of 16 FPS,
four denoising steps, and three latent frames per autoregressive chunk.
• Causal Forcing. We use BF16 precision, 832 × 480 resolution, a video frame rate of 16
FPS, four denoising steps, and three latent frames per autoregressive chunk.
B.2
DETAILS OF BASELINES
• Full KV. Full KV serves as the uncompressed reference and applies no additional cache evic-
tion. Its configuration should specify the backbone’s native attention mask, context window,
sink states, and re-caching behavior, so that the reference cache footprint is unambiguous.
• R-KV (Cai et al., 2025). R-KV selects cached tokens using importance and redundancy.
Its adaptation to video generation must specify the source of the importance scores, the
treatment of video-token positions, and the cache-update schedule. Any changes required
by chunk-wise denoising should be documented separately from the original selection rule.
• Dummy Forcing (Guo et al., 2026). Dummy Forcing allocates historical context according
to attention-head locality. Reproduction requires the head-classification procedure, profiling
inputs, retained context for each head category, and the frequency of classification. Profiling
and cache-layout conversion costs belong in the measured inference time.
• Forcing-KV (Ji et al., 2026). Forcing-KV applies different retention policies to static and
dynamic heads. The configuration should disclose the head-partition criterion, category-
specific retention settings, protected context, and compression schedule. The realized cache
footprint should be reported in addition to the method’s nominal compression parameters.
B.3
DETAILS OF BENCHMARKS
We evaluate video generation quality using benchmarks covering short-video quality, long-horizon
consistency, and human preference.
• VBench (Huang et al., 2024). VBench is a comprehensive benchmark for video generation
that evaluates 16 fine-grained dimensions covering visual quality, temporal consistency,
motion, and semantic alignment. We use all 946 official prompts for 5-second video
evaluation.
• VBenchLong (Huang et al., 2025f). VBenchLong extends fine-grained video evaluation
to long-duration generation, emphasizing temporal consistency, motion quality, and visual
stability over extended sequences. We evaluate 30-second and 60-second videos using 128
MovieGen prompts with five random seeds per prompt.
• VisionReward (Xu et al., 2026). VisionReward is a fine-grained human-preference reward
model for image and video generation. It evaluates multiple aspects of perceptual quality
and instruction alignment and aggregates them into an overall preference score. We follow
the Causal Forcing evaluation protocol and use its 100-prompt evaluation set.

Plan construction.
For each unique benchmark prompt, we prepare one plan and keep it fixed
during candidate generation. Given the original prompt q0 = q, the semantic planner returns the
semantic constraints R, prompt-specific exclusions N, and refined prompt q1 in a single call, as in
Eq. 3. The original prompt remains unchanged.
The semantic constraints R list the explicit content to preserve, including subjects, counts, attributes,
relations, actions, scenes, temporal cues, camera cues, styles, and the requested degree of motion.
The prompt-specific exclusions N identify omissions, contradictions, and unrequested additions to
avoid. The planner is instructed to rewrite q0 as a clear description that preserves the content in
R while avoiding the changes identified by N. It may clarify wording and composition, but must
not introduce new subjects, objects, actions, scene changes, on-screen text, viewpoints, or camera
motion. Explicit requests for stillness must be preserved, and a prompt that needs no refinement may
remain unchanged. The three outputs are produced together by the planner; the two sets guide the
rewrite rather than being concatenated to form q1. In particular, N is not supplied to the generator as
a separate negative prompt. Table 2 illustrates how a fragmented request can be rewritten without
adding new content.

Output checks and fallback.
A returned plan contains one to ten semantic constraints and at most

Setting and scope.
Fix one candidate, one completed-chunk update t, and one layer/KV-head

Relation to prior work and practical meaning.
Attention-output bounds and top-k bound mini-
mization have precedents in cache eviction (Feng et al., 2025a). Mean–maximum aggregation across
attention heads is also described in the OmniKV discussion of Hao et al. (2026). Here, the groups
come from planner-extracted semantic constraints, and the analysis identifies the smallest universal
additive bound for their individual discarded masses. Unlike the chunk-wise denoising analysis of
Light Forcing (Lv et al., 2026), the result requires neither a diffusion convergence rate nor a model of
how sparsity changes score-estimation error. It is a local guarantee for the observed groups and un-
changed KV entries. Whether cross-attention selects semantically informative queries, whether those
observations represent later queries, and whether the retained information improves video quality
remain empirical questions. No error tolerance, extra optimization term, or output-reconstruction
module is added to SEMANTICKV.
