## Abstract 

Large language models (LLMs) have been increasingly composed into multi-agent workflows to accomplish complex and multi-step tasks. As the open-weight models continue to close the performance gap with the proprietary ones, model parameters are becoming a less significant source of competitive advantage in the artificial intelligence market. Yet how these models are efficiently scheduled onto distributed infrastructure such as the cloud, with respect to overall latency, monetary cost and resource utilization, remains largely unaddressed in the literature despite being an increasingly critical problem. Existing agentic workflow frameworks mostly focus on output quality and task success rates over popular bench-marks, rarely accounting for execution dynamics of LLM-based agents such as inference latency, computation power of heteroge-neous resources, cloud-specific billing period and resource provi-sioning overheads. Therefore, this paper formulates multi-agent scheduling in cloud computing as a multi-objective optimization problem that jointly minimizes makespan and cost while maximiz-ing resource utilization. Building on this formulation, we propose MO-SAIC (Multi-Objective Scheduling of Multi-Agent Workflows in Cloud Computing), a scheduling framework comprising four multi-objective schedulers with Non-Dominated Sorting Genetic Algorithm II (NSGA-II), Weighted-Sum Genetic Algorithm (W-GA), a multi-objective adaptation of Heterogeneous Earliest Finish Time (MO-HEFT) and a random baseline (RAND). We evaluate the sched-ulers from the workflow scheduling, multi-objective optimization and system perspectives to measure their overall performance, qual-ity of their Pareto-optimal fronts and impacts of workflow topology, cloud resource composition and scalability parameters. We report the experimental results with their statistical significance via the Wilcoxon test. Lastly, we discuss the limitations of MO-SAIC as well as future research directions.

## 1 Introduction 

Consider an analogy from the history of cryptography. The field spent its early years as a state secret where advantage depended on which government could keep the cryptographic techniques hidden the longest through the mid-twentieth century. The decades since have reversed this trend, especially with the emergence of public-key cryptography. After that shift, security came to rest not on hiding the techniques but on protecting comparatively small secret keys. This transition follows the famous Kerckhoffs’s princi-ple [1], stating that a cryptosystem should not rely on the secrecy of the algorithm. We believe that large language models and agentic systems are currently heading toward an analogous transition. As open-weight models continue to close the performance gap with proprietary ones and model architectures are increasingly shared, model parameters are becoming a less significant source of compet-itive advantage. What increasingly differentiates is becoming not which models are used but how they are used at scale with respect to the efficient management of overall latency, cost and resource utilization. Rather than the model layer of artificial intelligence, the operational layer will be the harder challenge for large organiza-tions, making the scheduling of agentic workflows on distributed infrastructures an increasingly critical research problem. LLMs have rapidly progressed from static text generators to au-tonomous agents with advanced capabilities of planning, reasoning, invoking external tools and acting on their own. Further techniques for LLMs including tool-augmented generation [2] and interleaving of reasoning and acting [3] have enabled these agents to actively query APIs, execute code and retrieve information to complete complex tasks. They often chain together many such steps instead of acting on a single request in isolation. This conceptual shift in LLMs has also been documented across a rapidly growing body of survey literature [4]. However, all these autonomous actions con-sume real computational resources in the background, incurring latency and cost. As agents are integrated into production systems at increasing scale, their total operational footprint has become a significant concern to address in its own right. A single LLM-based agent is rarely sufficient for complex and multi-step tasks, which has led to composing multiple specialized agents into multi-agent workflows. An increasing number of works in the literature address the automation and optimization of this multi-agent workflow composition. For instance, AFlow [5] em-ploys a Monte Carlo tree search technique to iteratively sample, evaluate and refine different workflows. EvoFlow [6] instead evolves a diverse population of workflows based on an evolutionary algo-rithm with niching solution selection. However, once workflow topology has been fixed, no prior work has sufficiently addressed how its agents should be effectively assigned to and executed on physical cloud resources. In addition, while scheduling a single-agent system is relatively straightforward, the same is not true for multi-agent systems since such systems allow distinct agents to run on entirely separate resources. This gap motivates the problem of multi-agent workflow scheduling in distributed systems [7]. Scheduling traditional workflows across a pool of resources is not a new problem in the literature. Multi-objective evolutionary algorithms, most notably NSGA-II [8], have provided a technique to search for Pareto-optimal trade-offs when two or more objectives (e.g., makespan and cost) cannot be simultaneously minimized. arXiv:2611.13740v1 [cs.DC] 10 Nov 2026 In this respect, NSGA-II has been applied extensively to work-flow scheduling in cloud computing [9, 10] for the optimization of several objectives including makespan, cost, utilization, energy consumption, reliability and degree of imbalance. However, exist-ing scheduling techniques are primarily designed for traditional workflows (e.g., Montage in astronomy [11]), not for agentic work-flows where they do not consider the inherent characteristics of agents (e.g., inference time, tool calling time, output token size). Additionally, agents increasingly require heterogeneous CPU and GPU instances rather than a CPU-only pool. Figure 1 illustrates the distinction between the single-objective and multi-objective scheduling where the single-objective scheduler commits to a single solution purely via makespan without evaluating potential trade-offs even when it is extremely costly. The multi-objective scheduler instead offers multiple solutions that minimize latency, minimize cost or maximize resource utilization. This clearly shows that multi-agent workflow scheduling is inherently a multi-objective problem. Therefore, we position our work precisely at this intersection. 

## 1.1 Contributions 

This paper makes the following contributions to the literature: 

• A formal model of multi-agent workflow scheduling in cloud computing. We formulate multi-agent workflow scheduling in cloud computing as a multi-objective opti-mization problem by considering the minimization of work-flow makespan and monetary cost and the maximization of resource utilization. This problem abstracts four critical models: (i) a LLM-based agent model with inference latency, model-specific throughput, tool latency and output token size; (ii) a multi-agent workflow model through canonical agentic reasoning patterns including chain-of-thought, self-consistency, tree-of-thought, debate and self-refine; (iii) a heterogeneous cloud resource model by unifying CPU and GPU computation capacity; and (iv) a workflow simulation model to execute workflows on resources by considering cloud-specific issues such as hourly-billing and resource (de)provisioning overheads. 

• MO-SAIC – a multi-objective scheduling framework. 

We propose a novel multi-objective multi-agent workflow scheduling framework for cloud computing, MO-SAIC, with the total of four schedulers: (i) a non-dominated sorting ge-netic algorithm scheduler (NSGA-II) as Pareto metaheuristic; (ii) a weighted-sum genetic algorithm scheduler (W-GA) as a scalarized metaheuristic; (iii) a multi-objective HEFT sched-uler (MO-HEFT) as a classical list heuristic; and (iv) a random baseline scheduler (RAND) with no intelligence. 

• A controllable synthetic workflow generator. We em-ploy a parametric and synthetic multi-agent workflow gener-ator [12] to generate workflows with a diverse combination of canonical agentic reasoning patterns with given workflow size, depth and width. This generator allows controlled em-pirical study of how workflow topology affects scheduling decisions and quality. 

• Extensive experimental study. We extensively evaluate these four schedulers under various test scenarios to mea-sure: (i) their overall performance relative to a random base-line scheduler; (ii) quality of their Pareto-optimal fronts; (iii) effects of workflow topology with changing size, depth and width; and (iv) effects of cloud resources with resource size and CPU/GPU pool composition. In our experiments, we use several performance measures from three distinct perspec-tives: (i) optimization objectives, resource load imbalance, distinct resource count for the workflow scheduling per-spective; (ii) number of non-dominated solutions, Schott’s spacing, hypervolume and visual Pareto-optimal fronts for the multi-objective perspective and (iii) scheduling runtime for the system perspective. The statistical significance is shown via Wilcoxon rank-sum test across independent runs on distinct workflows. 

• A further discussion of limitations and future direc-tions. We also discuss the current assumptions and limi-tations of MO-SAIC against the practical requirements for production deployments and the potential research direc-tions to extend this research.

## 2.2 Multi-Objective Workflow Scheduling in Cloud Computing 

Multi-objective optimization has been extensively applied to work-flow scheduling in cloud computing, most commonly targeting the trade-off between makespan and monetary cost. Durillo et al. [9] adapt the classical HEFT heuristic [22] into MO-HEFT which is a Pareto-based list scheduling algorithm to maintain a set of partial scheduling solutions and measure its performance against synthetic and real-world workflows. Zhu et al. [23] instead propose a NSGA-II-based evolutionary multi-objective metaheuristic for the joint optimization of the makespan and cost. Li et al. [24] later improve the search performance of NSGA-II [8] for workflow scheduling through a scoring criterion and a dynamic hierarchical population. Ismayilov et al. [10, 25] extend multi-objective workflow schedul-ing to a dynamic setting so that it reacts better to environmental changes such as resource failures by incorporating a neural network into NSGA-II. They consider makespan, cost, utilization, energy, reliability and degree of imbalance as objectives. To minimize energy consumption together with makespan and cost, Yassa et al. [26] combine a hybrid particle swarm optimization with the dynamic voltage and frequency scaling technique. This enables the underlying processors to operate in different voltage supply levels by changing their clock frequencies. Later, Verma et al. [27] hybridize particle swarm optimization with a budget and deadline-constrained HEFT especially for scientific workflows. Mohammadzadeh et al. [28] propose another hybrid algorithm based on the seagull and grasshopper optimization algorithms to jointly optimize makespan, cost, energy and throughput, now with resources from multiple cloud providers. Abazari et al. [29] broaden the set of objectives further to include the minimization of security risk in the cloud and the response time. 

## 2.3 Model and Agent Scheduling for LLM Serving 

There exists a smaller and more recent group of works for schedul-ing of LLM executions across LLM engines. Luo et al. [30] propose Autellix which enriches schedulers with program-level statistics and minimizes end-to-end latency. Lin et al. [31] consider the data dependencies between consecutive LLM calls through semantic variables so that they can be co-scheduled. Jiang et al. [32] formally define the scheduling problem of LLM serving over a set of het-erogeneous GPU resources as a constrained optimization problem. Griggs et al. [33] assign LLM calls to GPU resources with distinct

## 3.3 Heterogeneous Cloud Model 

A cloud resource is a virtualized and on-demand computation in-stance to which an agent can be assigned for execution. Each re-source 𝑟 ∈ R is characterized by a tuple 𝑟 = (𝑣 𝑟 , 𝑔 𝑟 , 𝐵 𝑟 , 𝑝 𝑟 ), where 

𝑣 𝑟 ∈ Z≥0 is the vCPU count, 𝑔 𝑟 ∈ Z≥0 is the vGPU count, 𝐵 𝑟 ∈ R+

is the network bandwidth (Gbps) and 𝑝 𝑟 ∈ R+ is the hourly on-demand price (USD/hour). For the total compute power of a re-source, we unify CPU and GPU capacity into a single scalar via a weighted sum as: cp (𝑟 ) = 𝑤 𝑣 · 𝑣 𝑟 + 𝑤 𝑔 · 𝑔 𝑟 

where 𝑤 𝑣 , 𝑤 𝑔 ∈ R+ are the weights for the relative contribution of a vCPU and a vGPU to inference. For instance, a CPU-only resource ( 𝑔 𝑟 = 0) with 𝑤 𝑣 = 1 reduces cp (𝑟 ) to only its vCPU count exactly. From the available resources in the cloud, we compose three environments as CPU-only Rcpu with 𝑔 𝑟 = 0 for all 𝑟 , GPU-accelerated Rgpu with 𝑔 𝑟 > 0 for 𝑟 and CPU+GPU including all resources. When two dependent agents run on different resources, the output of the first agent must be transferred to the second over the network, subject to the available bandwidth. The bottleneck bandwidth between any two resources 𝑟 𝑖 , 𝑟 𝑗 ∈ R is computed as: 

𝐵 (𝑟 𝑖 , 𝑟 𝑗 ) = min (𝐵 𝑟 𝑖 , 𝐵 𝑟 𝑗 )

where 𝐵 (𝑟 𝑖 , 𝑟 𝑗 ) is the bandwidth between 𝑟 𝑖 and 𝑟 𝑗 . No network transfer occurs when two agents are hosted exactly on the same resource. In this work, we consider |R cpu | = 30 AWS EC2 CPU-only instances (see Table 5) and |R gpu | = 30 AWS EC2 GPU-accelerated instances (see Table 6). This resource catalog serves solely to param-eterize 𝑣 𝑟 , 𝑔 𝑟 , 𝐵 𝑟 and 𝑝 𝑟 with concrete values for our simulation and our cloud model itself remains independent of this particular set of resources. Notably, our unified computation model treats CPU and GPU capacity as interchangeable, which is very straightforward. This might result in assigning large models to CPU-only resources. Although such assignments are impractical in real executions, they are admissible under our model which does not intend to capture real executions. For the sake of simplicity, no workflow-driven or cloud-driven uncertainty (e.g., fluctuation in inference latency, network congestion) is considered in this work. 

3.3.1 CPU-Only Environment R ⊆ R cpu . This environment takes into account only the resources with no active GPU 𝑔 𝑟 = 0. This deliberate limitation on resource heterogeneity naturally results in certain scheduling impacts. First, unlike the GPU-only environ-ment, there is no hardware-driven improvement on latency and the schedulers cannot buy more speed for the critical-path agents and must instead rely more heavily on parallelizing agents across many resources. Second, per-hour prices of CPU-only resources are cheaper when compared to the resources with GPU, resulting in lower total resource costs at the end of the optimization. In ad-dition, CPU-only resources span a finer-grained range of vCPU counts and prices, which gives the schedulers a more fine-grained makespan–cost trade-off. In Table 5, the specifications of the CPU-only instance families (M1, M3, M4, M5, M5D) considered are listed, spanning vCPU counts from 1 to 96, network bandwidth from 10 to 25 Gbps and hourly pricing from $0.044 to $5.424. 

3.3.2 GPU-Accelerated Environment R ⊆ R gpu . Every resource in this environment is GPU-accelerated 𝑔 𝑟 > 0, offering more compu-tational power per unit than a CPU-only environment. Here, agents complete their inference faster, compressing makespan toward the lower values. However, this compression is not free since GPU-accelerated resources incur considerably higher per-hour costs in return. Therefore, this environment challenges the schedulers to still find a meaningful cost–utilization trade-off once the makespan objective is largely satisfied by hardware alone. In Table 6, the spec-ifications of the GPU-accelerated instance families (G4dn, G5, G6, G6e, G7, G7e, P4d, P4de, P5 and P5en) are listed, spanning several NVIDIA GPU generations, from the T4 through the H200. 

3.3.3 CPU+GPU Environment R ⊆ R cpu ∪ R gpu . This environment considers both CPU-only and GPU-accelerated resources at the same time for agent assignment. This provides a broader schedul-ing since organizations generally provision elastic cloud capacity in practice and relax the schedulers to make workload-specific de-cisions about which agent runs on which resource. In return, this hardens the overall optimization since the schedulers now have to map an agent on a cheaper but moderate-throughput CPU re-source or a more expensive but high-throughput GPU resource or their hybrid composition. For instance, an agent on the critical path might benefit more from a GPU to reduce latency for the agents immediately waiting on it. Consequently, achievable Pareto-fronts for this environment are likely to be more diverse, spanning the full range from all-CPU to all-GPU scheduling solutions and every combination in between.

## 4.1 Framework Architecture 

MO-SAIC is composed of five components: (i) workflow generator, generating individual agentic workflows with given size, depth and width parameters; (ii) cloud, providing the heterogeneous CPU and GPU resource pool to which the agents can be assigned; (iii) schedulers, searching the resulting decision space for the optimal scheduling solutions; (iv) simulator, executing candidate scheduling solutions to reveal the objective vector; and (v) evaluator, ranking the population of scheduling solutions by non-dominated sorting and crowding distance to reveal the Pareto-optimal front. For the fair comparison of the schedulers, we keep all other components identical across the framework. As a pipeline, the workflow gen-erator first generates a single workflow instance. Secondly, the schedulers take this instance and the available cloud resources to generate a population of scheduling solutions. Thirdly, regardless of which scheduler produced them, the simulator evaluates all so-lutions in the same way. Lastly, evaluator ranks the solutions to feed back to the schedulers until the maximum generation Γmax is reached and later to select the Pareto-optimal solutions. 

## 4.2 NSGA-II Scheduler 

NSGA-II [8] is an elitist multi-objective genetic algorithm that evolves a population of solutions across generations by using: (i) non-dominated sorting to rank solutions via Pareto-domination into several ranks; and (ii) crowding distance to preserve diversity in the same ranks. In this subsection, we customize NSGA-II to work with the solution representation and optimization objectives of our multi-objective agentic workflow scheduling problem. Refer to Algorithm 2 for the complete algorithm. 

4.2.1 Solution Representation. Each solution chromosome 𝜎 ∈ 𝑋 

is represented as a string of decision variables with the length of the number of available agents in workflow |A| . Each gene in a chro-mosome encodes the resource to which the agent on that position is assigned. During the execution of that agent (see Section 3.4), each gene is decoded to assign the agent to its corresponding resource. 

4.2.2 Population Initialization. The initial population of NSGA-II P0 = {𝜎 0, . . . , 𝜎 𝜆 −1 } is drawn by assigning every agent to an independent and uniformly random resource as: 

𝜎 𝑖 (𝛼 ) ∼ Uniform (R) , ∀ 𝛼 ∈ A , 𝑖 = 0, . . . , 𝜆 − 1where 𝜆 is the number of solutions in the population. All the sched-ulers in this framework are randomly initialized, which implies they all start from statistically indistinguishable initial populations. Therefore, every improvement reported in the experimental study can be directly attributed to the search space exploration capabili-ties of the schedulers themselves.

## 5.1 Benchmark Workflows 

Every workflow used in our experiments is generated by the para-metric and synthetic workflow generator [12] so that the workflow structure can be varied systematically and independently. ## 5.3 Statistical Analysis 

All experiments are conducted on a single machine of MacBook Air with Apple M2 chip, 8 GB memory and 8 CPU cores. We run every scheduler independently 20 times with freshly generated workflow instance and random seed for every run. Once a work-flow is generated, all schedulers are evaluated on this workflow. We collect the performance metrics per run and report their re-sults with their mean and standard deviations across these 20 runs. With independent measures, our goal is to eliminate the impact of randomness available in evolutionary computation and catch the typical behaviors of schedulers across distinct workflow instances, rather than a single specific draw. The RAND scheduler is just used as the baseline algorithm. The percentage improvements of the other schedulers with respect to RAND are reported where the pos-itive percentage means better than the baseline and vice versa. We perform the statistical significance test via the Wilcoxon rank-sum test and report significance at the conventional 𝑝 < 0.05 threshold. ## 6 Experimental Results 

This section reports the experimental results and the key findings. We discuss (i) the overall scheduling performance of the schedulers; (ii) their resulting Pareto-optimal fronts; (iii) the effects of workflow topology; and (iv) effects of heterogeneous cloud resources. 

## 6.1 Overall Scheduling Performance 

Table 3 reports the makespan, cost, utilization and Pareto-optimal front quality of all four schedulers at the default configuration of Table 2 as the average of 20 independent runs and Wilcoxon rank-sum significance. The first and most critical observation is that NSGA-II is the strongest scheduler that outperforms the other schedulers on most metrics by improving every objective. It lowers makespan by 8 .5% to 2663 .27 seconds, lowers cost by 94 .6% from $113.60 to $6.17 and raises utilization by 66 .8% from 30 .39% to 50 .69% while also improving resource imbalance by 35 .4%, the number of non-dominated solutions by 104 .9% and hypervolume by 185 .1%. No other scheduler except W-GA improves makespan, cost and utilization at the same time. 

Cost is the objective that the schedulers separate most and makespan the least. Cost spans over twenty-fold across the four schedulers from NSGA-II’s $6.17 to MO-HEFT’s $125.49. On the other hand, the schedulers except RAND stay within about 3% of each other on makespan from 2663 .27 to 2731 .46 seconds. Figure 3 shows that the inference part of each agent scaling with the resource it runs on is narrower than the tool latency, which leaves little room to minimize makespan. The hourly price of a resource, by contrast, varies widely across Table 5 and Table 6. Hence, the search moves cost and utilization the most. MO-HEFT reaches a high utilization, but without reaching it economically. Its utilization of 49 .84% is close to NSGA-II’s 50 .69% and it has the lowest resource imbalance at 1 .49. However, its cost of $125.49 is the worst of all four schedulers. W-GA improves cost and utilization but narrows the front. It reduces cost by 59 .1% and raises utilization by 18 .4%, but its count of non-dominated solutions falls to 8 .50 with a Schott’s spacing of 0 .03. 

6.1.1 Generation Count. Figure 10 in Appendix E traces the best makespan, cost and utilization found by W-GA and NSGA-II over 5000 generations of a single run. Cost improves earliest and most sharply for both schedulers. W-GA reduces the best cost from about $123.00 to about $33.00 within the first thirty generations and NSGA-II reduces it from about $146.00 to about $25.00 within the first two hundred. The initial population assigns every agent to an independent uniformly-random resource and therefore contains many expensive instances that need to be removed. After that, both schedulers continue to lower cost slowly where W-GA reaches $23.70 and NSGA-II reaches $4.88 at generation 5000. Makespan converges early and improves little afterwards. W-GA settles around 2575 seconds by generation 600 and improves only to 2570 seconds for the remaining 4400 generations. NSGA-II settles around 2735 seconds by generation 1100 and later brings it to 2707 seconds. 

Utilization is the slowest objective to converge for NSGA-II. 

W-GA raises utilization from about 34% to about 42% by generation 1350 and then remains flat. NSGA-II instead climbs continuously, passing 50% near generation 750, reaching 55% near generation 2350 and completing at 56.5%. NSGA-II keeps discovering improve-ments later than W-GA. The utilization of W-GA stops changing after roughly generation 1350 while its cost and makespan improve intermittently where the last of which occurs near generation 3750. By contrast, NSGA-II records a makespan step near generation 4200 and utilization gains past generation 4600. Selection of generation cut point forms a trade-off between scheduling quality and runtime. 

6.1.2 Temporal Scheduling Overhead. Figure 8 in Appendix D re-ports the wall-clock scheduling time of the four schedulers in MO-SAIC while Figure 9 specifically focuses on NSGA-II for the growing population and workflow size. RAND is trivially fast with 0.0007 seconds in total. This is well expected since RAND performs only a single dependency-aware topological sort followed by a random resource draw. On the other hand, MO-HEFT, W-GA and NSGA-II take 203 .53, 73 .56 and 96 .26 seconds, respectively. MO-HEFT is the most computationally expensive scheduler. For each of the 𝑁 = 50 agents, it extends every 𝜆 = 50 solution with every 

|R| = 60 default resource choice, producing 𝜆 |R| = 3000 candidate extensions that must be reduced back down to 𝜆 solutions through the non-dominated sorting and crowding-distance operators. 

The heaviest computational overhead of MO-HEFT does not mean consistently superior scheduling quality. According to Table 3, MO-HEFT delivers a 7 .0% makespan gain over RAND and a worse cost of $125 .49 in return for significant gains in utiliza-tion. In contrast, NSGA-II achieves significant improvements over RAND, making it favorable for the balance between computational overhead and scheduling quality. Applying non-dominated sort-ing of NSGA-II has a measurable overhead over weighted-sum of W-GA. This is especially visible between W-GA’s 73 .5614 seconds and NSGA-II’s 96 .2599 seconds while they both run the identical population size and generation count. While W-GA em-ploys a single weighted-sum fitness for solutions, NSGA-II performs non-dominated sorting and crowding distance at every generation.

## 6.2 Pareto Frontier Analysis 

This section presents three two-dimensional space projections of makespan–cost, makespan–utilization and cost–utilization from the three-dimensional objective space in Figure 11 for visual inspection. 

6.2.1 Makespan–Cost Frontier. The scheduling solutions in Ta-ble 3 are distributed more widely along the cost axis than along the makespan axis. Cost ranges over twenty-fold across the four schedulers, from NSGA-II’s $6.17 to MO-HEFT’s $125.49. However, makespan varies within about 3% across the schedulers except RAND, from NSGA-II’s 2663 .27 to W-GA’s 2731 .46 seconds, with RAND higher at 2911 .37 seconds. Notably, NSGA-II dominates the other schedulers by finding the lowest cost and the lowest makespan. A spacing pattern is also visible in Figure 11d where so-lutions are clustered toward the low-cost and low-makespan corner, unlike those in Figure 11a in Appendix F. 

6.2.2 Cost–Utilization Frontier. Two most utilization-optimal schedulers act differently on the cost axis. MO-HEFT and NSGA-II reach roughly the same utilization levels of 49 .84% and 50 .69%, respectively. However, MO-HEFT’s cost of $125.49 is twenty times higher than NSGA-II’s $6.17, showing that MO-HEFT does not use resources economically. It suggests that reaching a utiliza-tion level offers no guarantee of reaching it economically, as seen in Figure 11b and Figure 11d in Appendix F. Moreover, W-GA im-proves on RAND by raising utilization from 30 .39% to 35 .97% while reducing cost from $113.60 to $46.46. 

6.2.3 Makespan–Utilization Frontier. A richer front does not necessarily mean a more evenly-distributed trade-off for makespan–utilization. NSGA-II carries the most non-dominated solutions of the four schedulers with 25 .20 against RAND’s 12 .30. However, its Schott’s spacing of 0 .10 is worse than RAND’s 0 .06, indicating that its larger front is not uniformly distributed, as seen in Figure 11d. W-GA shows the opposite pattern where it has fewer non-dominated solutions than even RAND, 8 .50 against 12 .30 while it has the tightest spacing of all four schedulers at 0 .03. Its weighted-sum fitness directs solutions toward a narrow and tightly-clustered region of the makespan–utilization space. 

## 6.3 Influence of Workflow Topology on NSGA-II 

This section evaluates the influence of workflow topology including size, depth and width on NSGA-II scheduling performance. 

6.3.1 Workflow Size. Table 7 reports mean experimental results over 20 independent runs as the workflow size 𝑁 grows from 25 to 120 agents. Cost grows faster than makespan as workflow size increases. From 𝑁 = 25 to 𝑁 = 120, makespan rises by only about a third from 2292 .91 to 3057 .72 seconds while cost shows a dramatic rise of 44-fold from $1.2469 to $55.2051. Since workflow depth is fixed, the additional agents are included within the existing dependency levels rather than lengthening the critical path. Hence, the growing workload is executed largely in parallel, not affecting makespan to a great degree. In contrast, cost, as the sum of billing durations, accumulates roughly with each agent added and grows correspondingly faster than makespan. 

NSGA-II absorbs the growing workload by recruiting more distinct resources at the expense of utilization and load imbal-ance. The number of distinct resources grows more than threefold from 13 .39 at 𝑁 = 25 to 44 .01 at 𝑁 = 120 while utilization gradually falls from 56 .93% to 40 .20% and resource imbalance worsens from 1.3891 to 2 .1362. A larger 𝑁 causes more agents at the same depen-dency levels to be ready at once and NSGA-II spreads them across a wider set of resources to avoid delays and keep makespan small. However, each resource comes with its hourly-rounded cycle that rarely aligns with how long that agent is actually busy, which low-ers the average busy-time per resource as the total amount of work grows. Therefore, workflow size trades utilization and resource imbalance for bounded makespan growth. 

Increase in hypervolume does not necessarily reflect im-provement with a stronger front. Hypervolume climbs from 0.7466 at 𝑁 = 25 to 0 .9979 at 𝑁 = 100, which would suggest an improvement on its own at the first glance. However, a larger workflow also widens the achievable range for makespan, cost and utilization, which can raise the hypervolume without an actual improvement. At the same time, the number of non-dominated solutions declines from 32 .50 at 𝑁 = 25 to roughly 21 to 24 for 

𝑁 ≥ 75 and Schott’s spacing worsens from 0 .0843 to 0 .1093. This suggests that NSGA-II yields a sparser and less evenly-distributed front. Workflow size also inflates the computational cost of scheduling itself. Figure 9 in Appendix D reports the wall-clock computation time with the increasing workflow size. As shown, the runtime grows with 𝑁 since every additional agent lengthens the solution length and adds to the simulation complexity for every candidate solution at every generation. When evaluated together with the degrading multi-objective performance, workflow size

This compression is also visible in Schott’s spacing that decreases from 0 .0998 to 0 .0724 between 𝑑 (𝐺 ) = 15 and 𝑑 (𝐺 ) = 35. 

6.3.3 Workflow Parallelism. Table 9 in Appendix G reports how NSGA-II scheduling performance changes as width 𝑤 (𝐺 ) grows. Width sets the largest number of agents that can share a single dependency level. A wider workflow packs the same 50 agents into fewer and wider levels of simultaneous agents. ## 6.4 Influence of Cloud Resources on NSGA-II 

This section evaluates the influence of cloud resources including resource size and CPU/GPU composition on NSGA-II scheduling performance. 

6.4.1 Resource Size. Table 10 in Appendix H reports the way NSGA-II scheduling performance changes as the cloud resource pool grows from |R| = 60 to 180 and 300 while the workflow topology stays fixed. Makespan is insensitive to the size of the resource pool. 

It moves from 2663 .27 seconds at |R| = 60 to 2676 .70 at |R| = 180 and 2591 .85 at |R| = 300 without a visible trend. For the workflow size of 𝑁 = 50, available resources are sufficient and adding more does not reduce makespan. However, resource size might be a bot-tleneck for larger workflows. A larger pool inflates cost and lowers utilization. Cost rises from $6.1716 at |R| = 60 to $17.8053 at |R| = 180 and $25.7325 at |R| = 300 while utilization falls from 50 .69% to 45 .67% and resource imbalance worsens from 1 .6284 to 1.8114. The number of distinct resources also grows from 27 .00 to 39 .88. The Pareto-front does not improve with a larger pool. 

The number of non-dominated solutions rises only slightly from 25 .20 to 28 .50, Schott’s spacing improves marginally from 0 .0998 to 0 .0839 and hypervolume stays near 0 .86 with no clear trend. Overall, enlarging the resource pool beyond the concurrency the workflow requires brings no benefit to makespan or to front quality while it measurably worsens cost and utilization. 

6.4.2 Environment Heterogeneity. Table 11 in Appendix H reports NSGA-II scheduling performance when the resource pool is re-stricted to CPU-only or GPU-only instances against the default CPU+GPU environment. The GPU-only pool gives the lowest makespan. Makespan falls to 2620 .16 seconds against 2774 .33 for CPU-only and 2663 .27 for CPU+GPU, about 6% below the CPU-only setting. GPU acceleration raises cp (𝑟 ) and shortens the inference term of every agent. The GPU-only pool costs roughly ten times more. Cost reaches $64.7780 against $6.7696 for CPU-only and $6.1716 for CPU+GPU. GPU instances carry much higher hourly prices and an all-GPU pool leaves no cheap fallback. CPU+GPU reaches the lowest cost of the three because it can place most agents on cheap CPU instances and use GPUs only where they help, under our simplified computation power model. 

CPU-only reaches the highest utilization and CPU+GPU has the most non-dominated solutions. Utilization is 53 .86% for CPU-only, 50 .69% for CPU+GPU and 46 .84% for GPU-only since fast GPU execution leaves resources idle within their billing hour. The number of non-dominated solutions is 25 .20 for CPU+GPU against 23 .00 for CPU-only and 15 .60 for GPU-only and Schott’s spacing is best for CPU+GPU at 0 .0998. Both resource classes together give NSGA-II the widest set of makespan–cost trade-offs to populate. The GPU-only front collapses once makespan is satisfied by hardware 

> 15

alone. Overall, the mixed CPU+GPU environment delivers near the GPU-only makespan at the CPU-only cost with the most non-dominated solutions. 

## 7 Further Discussions 

MO-SAIC makes a number of design and implementation assump-tions to keep the problem tractable. In this section, we discuss such further issues as well as potential future research directions: (1) Synthetic workflows rather than realistic agentic traces. 

The workflows in this paper are generated through our syn-thetic and parametric workflow generator [12] by compiling several agentic reasoning patterns under a Markovian transition matrix. Admittedly, this generator enables us to vary workflow size, depth and width independently and study their impacts in isolation. However, it might not fully reflect how real-world organizations compose their workflows in practice. In the fu-ture, we aim to collect realistic agentic workflow traces and re-evaluate our schedulers against them. (2) A simulated but not measured execution environment. 

Agent and cloud characteristics including inference latency, tool latency, provisioning and deprovisioning delays and net-work bandwidth are modeled in this work, by using static AWS pricing and LLM catalogs and Gaussian noise, rather than real-world measurements taken from cloud providers. However, real environment execution might show variability from network congestion, cold-start delays and interference, which can affect makespan. Deploying and simulating the MO-SAIC schedulers on an actual cloud environment is a promising direction. (3) A simplified computation power unification for CPUs and GPUs. Our computation power model combines vCPU and vGPU counts through a single fixed-weighted sum cp (𝑟 ) =

𝑤 𝑣 𝑣 𝑟 +𝑤 𝑔 𝑔 𝑟 where it treats every vGPU unit as equally powerful regardless of GPU generation. However, in reality, a T4 and an H200 differ for the same workload. A natural next step is to replace this linear abstraction with per-generation throughput multipliers for more realistic workflow simulations. (4) Abstracting away hardware compatibility. MO-SAIC per-mits any agent to be assigned to any cloud resource, so a sched-uler might place a large model onto a CPU-only instance whose memory could not actually hold the model weights. However, in practice, model weights impose minimum memory footprint that only certain resources can satisfy. Some models might re-quire multiple GPUs together, rather than a single resource. Consequently, some of the solutions our schedulers generate as optimal might not be deployed on real infrastructure. There-fore, incorporating per-model memory requirements into the assignment constraints might be a critical future direction for a production-ready MO-SAIC. (5) A static and offline scheduling formulation. This paper considers the multi-objective optimization problem as time-invariant over fixed workflows and fixed cloud resources. How-ever, real deployments might face changes over time, such as software and hardware failures. In addition, agents might fail and retry in tool calling. The schedulers in this paper are not capable of reacting to these scenarios effectively. Therefore, ex-tending our work to a dynamic multi-objective problem would be beneficial for more adaptive multi-objective scheduling. (6) Set of optimization objectives. MO-SAIC optimizes makespan, cost and utilization objectives by remaining entirely agnostic to the correctness of model responses. However, scheduling depending on smaller models might degrade the quality of that agent contribution even if it reduces cost and latency. This trade-off is not addressed where introducing task success probability or response quality as a further objective might be taken into consideration. In addition, energy consumption might be also studied in the future together with the existing objectives. 

## 8 Conclusion 

In this paper, we formulate LLM-based multi-agent workflow sched-uling in cloud computing as a multi-objective optimization problem, jointly considering the minimization of makespan and cost and the maximization of resource utilization. For the given problem, we propose MO-SAIC, a multi-objective scheduling framework with a total of four schedulers. To evaluate the schedulers in MO-SAIC, we employ a parametric and synthetic multi-agent workflow gener-ator to systematically vary workflow topology with respect to size, depth and width. We measure performance under the extensive test scenarios as: (i) their overall performance improvements relative to RAND; (ii) quality of their fronts; (iii) impacts of workflow topol-ogy; and (iv) impacts of cloud resources. The performance metrics we use span three main perspectives as: (i) objective functions, resource load imbalance, distinct resource count for the workflow scheduling perspective; (ii) number of non-dominated solutions, Schott’s spacing, hypervolume and visual Pareto-optimal fronts for the multi-objective perspective; and (iii) scheduling runtime for the system perspective. Lastly, we identify the current limita-tions of MO-SAIC and the potential future research directions. We expect that, rather than the model layer, the operational layer of artificial intelligence will be increasingly important in the future as the open-weight models close the performance gap with the proprietary ones, making the multi-objective multi-agent workflow scheduling a critical problem to address for large organizations.
