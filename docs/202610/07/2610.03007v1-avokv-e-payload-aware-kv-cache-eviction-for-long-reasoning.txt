Abstract 

Long-output reasoning shifts the KV-cache bottleneck from the fixed prompt to the generated trace. Existing reasoning-cache eviction methods largely treat cached entries as routing objects, estimating whether an old key will still be read, will recur, or can be replaced. This routing-only view overlooks two effects: low-attention entries can carry large value payloads whose removal changes future predictions, and newly generated states can appear stale before later queries have had a chance to read them. We introduce AvoKV-E, a training-free eviction policy that first delays eligibility for recent states and then ranks eligible entries using candidate-normalized read pressure, key redundancy, and value-payload potential. According to empirical evaluation across different models and datasets, AvoKV-E matches or exceeds redundancy-aware, recurrence-based, and thought-adaptive eviction baselines at matched active-KV budgets, with its largest gains in the tightest-cache regime. Component and counterfactual analyses further connect these gains to delayed observation, payload-aware scoring, redundancy, and scale-robust normalization. Together, the results show that long-reasoning KV eviction should preserve not only keys that are likely to be read, but also the value payloads that sustain the reasoning trajectory. 

# 1 Introduction 

Large reasoning models such as DeepSeek-R1 (DeepSeek-AI, 2025) and Qwen3 (Yang et al., 2025) often solve difficult problems by producing long derivations, intermediate calculations, and self-corrections. Every generated token appends a key and a value at every layer, turning the reasoning trace into a growing memory object. Under a tight active-cache budget, an eviction mistake can therefore disrupt the reasoning process itself: the model may revisit completed work, enter a loop, or fail to produce a final answer. Most KV-cache policies decide what to retain by asking whether an entry is likely to be retrieved again. Attention histories, recurrence, redundancy, and thought-level structure provide different signals for answer-ing this question (Liu et al., 2023; Oren et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2023; Xiao et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024; Cai et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025; Ramachandran et al., 2025; Su et al., 2026; Hu et al., 2025). At a high level, however, these methods primarily evaluate the cache through its keys: will an entry receive future attention, or can a nearby key serve as a substitute? This routing-oriented view misses two properties that matter in long reasoning. First, a KV entry has two distinct roles: its key acts as a retrieval address, while its value contains the vector returned when that address is read. Eviction removes both, so an entry that appears unimportant from its attention pattern can still carry consequential content. Second, a newly generated state may appear unimportant simply because later queries have not yet had an opportunity to use it. At matched active-KV budgets, it matches or outperforms redundancy-aware, recurrence-based, and thought-adaptive baselines, with its largest gains in the tightest-cache regime. Component ablations and counter-factual oracle analysis further connect the end-task improvements to delayed observation, payload-aware scoring, redundancy, and candidate-relative normalization. 

Contributions. Our contributions are three-fold: • We identify two limitations of routing-only eviction in long reasoning: it can overlook consequential value payloads and evaluate recent states before their utility becomes observable. • We introduce AvoKV-E, a training-free policy that combines delayed eligibility with candidate-normalized read pressure, key redundancy, and value-payload potential. • We evaluate AvoKV-E across two reasoning models and two mathematical reasoning benchmarks, and analyze its components, oracle ranking quality, and termination behavior. 22 Related Work 

Reasoning-cache compression. Long-output reasoning makes the generated trace itself a dominant KV-cache object. Recent methods adapt compression to this setting using reasoning milestones or current-query importance (Hu et al., 2025; Su et al., 2026), key redundancy (Cai et al., 2025), lagged recurrence of attention importance (Zhang et al., 2025), or thought-adaptive compression across layers (Ramachandran et al., 2025). As an online eviction policy for generated reasoning states, AvoKV-E is closest to R-KV and LazyEviction. R-KV asks whether a key is important and non-redundant, while LazyEviction asks whether attention importance recurs after a lag. In contrast, the delay in AvoKV-E determines when an entry becomes eligible rather than acting as an importance score; once eligible, the entry is evaluated for the risk of discarding both its routing address and its value content. The method therefore requires neither a reasoning-milestone detector nor a thought-regime classifier. 

Attention-based retention and observation. A broader line of KV-cache compression estimates future utility from attention history. Scissorhands exploits the persistence of token importance, H2O preserves attention heavy hitters, StreamingLLM protects attention sinks, and TOVA updates the cache using recent attention (Liu et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2023; Xiao et al., 2023; Oren et al., 2024). SnapKV and NACL instead aggregate statistics over a short observation window before selecting retained entries (Li et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024). Although their retention rules differ, these methods primarily evaluate an entry as an attention destination. CAOTE moves closer to the consequence of eviction by estimating attention-output error (Goel et al., 2025). AvoKV-E shares this concern, but uses an online value-norm proxy inside a reasoning-cache loop and delays the scoring of recent states whose future use has not yet become observable. 

Budget allocation and query-aware access. A complementary family decides where limited capacity should be spent across layers, heads, or queries (Ge et al., 2024; Cai et al., 2024; Feng et al., 2024; Fu et al., 2024; Tang et al., 2024; Xiao et al., 2024). These methods change the allocation or access structure of the cache rather than only the score used to rank individual entries under a fixed budget. Our study holds allocation uniform across layers and heads so that it can isolate the token-level eviction decision. Within this controlled setting, AvoKV-E asks which generated reasoning states should remain once the available capacity has been specified. 

Quantization and serving systems. KV-cache quantization reduces the memory occupied by retained states through lower or adaptive precision (Liu et al., 2024; Hooper et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025; Zandieh et al., 2025), while PagedAttention improves how KV memory is managed by the serving system (Kwon et al., 2023). These techniques address storage precision and memory organization, respectively, and are complementary to the eviction policy studied here. We retain full-precision entries and use a shared decoding harness so that the experiments compare which reasoning states are kept rather than differences in quantization or serving infrastructure.

## 4.1 Delayed Causal Observation 

Motivation. A newly generated state may receive little attention simply because only a few subsequent queries have been available to read it. Scoring such a state immediately would conflate “not yet observed” with “unimportant.” AvoKV-E therefore delays the eviction decision until the state has remained in the cache long enough to be observed causally. 

Cache partition. At checkpoint t, let Wobs be the number of recent query positions used to measure read pressure, and let Wdelay ≥ Wobs be the period during which a newly generated entry remains protected. We define 

Ot = {τ : t − Wobs < τ ≤ t}, (2) 

Pt = {i : t − Wdelay < i ≤ t}, (3) 

Ct = {i : i ≤ t − Wdelay }. (4) 

Roles of the three sets. The observation set Ot contains the recent query positions used for scoring. The protected set Pt contains newly generated entries that cannot yet be evicted, whereas the candidate set Ct

contains older entries that are eligible for ranking. Only entries in Ct compete for the remaining cache budget after Pt has been reserved. 

Effect of the delay. Because Wdelay ≥ Wobs , every candidate in Ct predates every query in Ot. An entry is therefore exposed to eviction only after the queries used to measure its read pressure have had a causal opportunity to attend to it. The delay does not itself predict importance, as a recurrence model does (Zhang et al., 2025); it is a measurement rule that controls eligibility. Once an entry leaves Pt, the payload-aware score determines whether a low-read state should nevertheless be retained because it carries a large value payload.

Interpreting the components. Read pressure pi averages the attention received from queries in Ot and is high when the current context continues to read the entry. Redundancy ri compares the entry with its candidate neighborhood Ni in key space and is high when a nearby substitute exists. Payload potential bi

compares the value norm with the per-layer/head median over Ct, providing a candidate-relative proxy for how much value content the entry carries. The three components thus answer different questions: whether the entry is read, whether its key is replaceable, and whether its value payload is salient. Crucially, AvoKV-E does not multiply bi by pi. Such a product would suppress exactly the low-read/high-payload states identified in Figure 1 (a). Keeping the payload term independent allows an unusually large value vector to rescue a candidate even when its recent attention is low. This term is related in spirit to CAOTE’s attention-output error (Goel et al., 2025), but instantiates that intuition as an online value-norm proxy within the delayed reasoning-cache loop. 

Candidate-relative normalization. The three raw components cannot be combined directly because they have different numerical scales. We therefore express each component by its percentile rank among the eligible candidates at the current checkpoint: 

Nt(xi) = rank Ct (xi) − 1

|Ct| − 1 , (8) where rank Ct is ascending and ties are averaged. The smallest candidate value therefore maps to 0, the largest maps to 1, and all three components are placed on the same [0, 1 ] scale. We then combine the normalized components into the eviction-risk score 

Si(t) = λp Nt(pi) − λr Nt(ri) + λb Nt(bi). (9) The signs encode the role of each component. High read pressure and high value-payload potential make eviction riskier and therefore increase Si(t), whereas high key redundancy makes eviction safer and decreases it. An entry can thus be retained because it is actively read, difficult to replace, or carries an unusually salient payload. The protected initial entries remain in Ct but receive Si(t)= + ∞, ensuring that the top-score selection always retains them. We protect the first four cache entries in all experiments. Given active budget B, AvoKV-E first reserves the protected set Pt and fills the remaining B − | Pt| slots with the highest-scoring candidates: Kt = Pt ∪ Top B−| Pt |{Si(t) : i ∈ Ct}. We use λp=0.1, λr =0.9, and λb=0.05 throughout the main experiments and ablate each component in §5.3.

Every quantity used at checkpoint t is already available in the decoded prefix. Read pressure uses only attention rows from queries in Ot, redundancy compares stored RoPE-transformed keys in Ct, and payload potential uses stored value norms. ## 5.3 Why Does AvoKV-E Work? 

Counterfactual oracle ranking. To isolate the score from downstream decoding, we evaluate several scoring rules against single-token oracle eviction-KL labels for 5,440 aligned candidate KV entries from 30 DeepSeek-R1 AIME-2024 traces. We report AUROC, average precision (AP), top-k recall, false-safe rate, and Spearman correlation. This analysis complements the leave-one-out KL diagnostic in App. D. The full AvoKV-E score is strongest on every ranking metric. The largest improvement appears in top-k

recall, which most directly measures how many high-damage entries the policy preserves. Because this 9comparison is independent of the decoding loop and uses the same runtime budget for all proxy scores, it links the downstream accuracy gains to the proposed payload-aware ranking rule. The five metrics probe complementary aspects of the same ranking problem. AUROC and Spearman measure global ordering, while AP and top-k recall emphasize the high-damage candidates that should remain near the top of the keep list. The false-safe rate measures the converse error: damaging entries assigned to the apparently safe-to-evict region. AvoKV-E improves all five simultaneously, rather than improving one portion of the ranking at the expense of another. This is particularly relevant to online eviction, where mistakes near the keep–evict boundary become irreversible once an entry is removed. 

Component ablations. The largest ablation loss at every tested budget comes from removing key re-dundancy. This clarifies the role of payload awareness: AvoKV-E does not replace routing evidence, but augments a strong routing backbone where that evidence is incomplete. Removing the payload term still lowers accuracy at every budget, consistent with the low-read/high-payload blindspot in Figure 1. Remov-ing delayed eligibility also consistently hurts, as expected when recent states can be judged before later queries have had an opportunity to read them. Payload awareness and delayed eligibility therefore correct two different limitations of routing-only eviction: what evidence is considered and when that evidence becomes trustworthy. The raw-score ablation shows that candidate-relative normalization is not merely a cosmetic rescaling. Read pressure, redundancy, and payload potential occupy different numerical ranges, so combining them directly changes their effective balance even when the nominal weights remain fixed. The degradation across all three budgets supports the scale-robust construction in Eq. 8. Separately, the in-runner R-KV anchor closely tracks the official R-KV results, making a harness mismatch an unlikely explanation for the improvement of AvoKV-E.

# 6 Conclusion 

This paper addresses KV-cache compression for long-output reasoning with AvoKV-E, a training-free policy that separates delayed eligibility from payload-aware eviction risk. Across two reasoning models and two mathematical reasoning benchmarks, AvoKV-E matches or exceeds the tested redundancy-aware, recurrence-based, and thought-adaptive baselines at matched active-KV budgets, with its largest gains under the tightest budgets. The oracle ranking and component analyses connect these gains to preserving high-damage value payloads, allowing late reads to materialize, and normalizing heterogeneous signals within the candidate set. More broadly, the results suggest that reasoning-cache eviction should preserve both retrievable keys and the value payloads that sustain the reasoning trajectory. 10 Limitations 

We evaluate two reasoning models (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B and Qwen3-14B) on two mathematical reasoning benchmarks (MATH-500 and AIME-25); we do not claim broad task coverage and have not tested code or multimodal reasoning. The prototype runs in HuggingFace generation rather than a serving kernel, so we report active KV budgets and decoding behavior, not packed-memory savings or end-to-end throughput. AvoKV-E is an eviction policy; evaluating it alongside low-bit KV quantization and hybrid eviction–quantization allocation is a natural next step.

# C Additional Reproducibility Details 

The evaluation protocol, baseline configurations, compression schedule, and fixed AvoKV-E configuration are described in §5.1. Here we record the causal availability of the scoring signals, the pilot observations behind the fixed configuration, and the terms of the models and datasets used in the evaluation. 

Causal scoring. All three signals consume only information already in the prefix at checkpoint t. The read-pressure feature averages attention rows from queries in Ot over layers and heads; the redundancy feature compares RoPE-transformed keys within the candidate set Ct; the payload feature depends only on stored value norms. No future tokens, oracle labels, or auxiliary networks are used. This is what makes the score deployable inside ordinary autoregressive decoding. 

Configuration-selection rationale. The MATH-100/MATH-200 pilot pass yielded three observations that motivated the fixed choices in §5.1. First, the protected tail scales with the budget: at budget 1024, a short delay Wdelay =8 is optimal, whereas at budget 512, a longer delay Wdelay =32 is consistently more stable because tighter budgets evict more aggressively and need a wider window to absorb late first reads. Second, the payload weight is small but non-zero: setting λb=0 costs several points across budgets, while pushing λb

much beyond ∼ 0.05 degrades behavior. The payload term is intended to break ties among similarly routed 14 candidates and rescue the high-payload states identified by the attention-only blindspot, not to dominate the score; the relatively large redundancy weight λr =0.9 keeps the overall score close to the strong R-KV family. Third, percentile-rank and age-bucket-rank normalization are interchangeable in the pilots, whereas the scale-sensitive normalizers we tried (raw, z-score, min–max, max-absolute, and softmax) collapse the score toward attention-only behavior and forfeit 3–11 points depending on budget. Under the same harness, we also compared the full AvoKV-E score against R-KV+payload, a recency-weighted variant, and an R-KV-style anchor; the composite score was consistently best at both pilot budgets.

# D Empirical foundations of Figure 1 

Two intuitions underlie AvoKV-E: that attention magnitude alone is a lossy summary of a token’s importance because it ignores the size of what the token would actually contribute if read, and that a single recent observation window is too short a horizon to reliably classify reasoning tokens as useful or stale. Figure 1 presents these ideas in takeaway form; this appendix gives the detailed construction behind the figure, including the candidate pool, the oracle high-damage definition, the attention/value-norm bins in panel (a), the strong-read lag statistic in panel (b), and the significance tests behind the reported blindspot. 

Candidate pool. We pool n=892 candidate KV entries from five MATH/AIME traces generated by DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B. A candidate is a (trace, position) pair whose remaining horizon supports the longest measurement window required below ( Wfut =8 steps for oracle damage, up to 32 steps for the lag analysis). The candidates are not subsampled within a trace, so the marginals on the panel-(a) heatmap reflect the actual distribution of attention and value-norm ranks under decoded reasoning, not an attention-stratified subset. Each candidate is described by the two within-trace percentile ranks introduced in §4: recent attention Nt(pi), aggregated over the last Wobs decode steps (Eq. 5); and value-norm Nt(bi)= ∥vi∥2,the ℓ2 magnitude of the candidate’s stored value vector — the v from QKV that would be returned via attention if the entry were retained (Eq. 7).

Delayed first reads and the case for delayed observation (panel b). The blindspot exists because attention-only scoring is a snapshot at a single moment. Whether that snapshot is informative depends on how quickly reasoning tokens are read after generation. We define a strong read of candidate i as a future query position τ

whose attention ατ,i reaches at least 0.85 of i’s per-trace peak attention, and the first-strong-read lag of i as the smallest such τ − i. Lags are right-censored at 32 decode steps, and we restrict the analysis to the n=542 candidates whose remaining horizon supports that cap so the distribution is not artificially truncated. The diagnostic quantity is the cumulative read probability F(t)= P(lag ≤t), the fraction of candidates that have been strongly read by lag t. Splitting candidates by attention quartile ( Nt(QK ⊤)≤0.4 vs. Nt(QK ⊤)≥0.6; 

n=218 each), F(4)= 51% vs. 62% and F(16 )= 56% vs. 72%; the high-attention curve dominates throughout and the gap to the low-attention curve widens with t (one-sided Kolmogorov–Smirnov p=0.010). The plateau is informative on its own: 15% of low-attention candidates are never strongly read inside the 32-step horizon at all. Reading this from the eviction side, an attention-only policy that decides at small t is precisely the policy that conflates “not yet read” with “not useful”, and the worst-case lag is concentrated on exactly the low-attention candidates the policy is most eager to discard. Waiting more decode steps before the decision — the delayed causal observation construction in §4 — allows these late reads to materialize, costing only bookkeeping but turning the gap in panel (b) into a reliable keep/evict signal. Together, panels (a) and (b) make a complementary case: the snapshot attention used by prior eviction methods is structurally blind to a high-damage subpopulation (panel a), and it is unreliable at the short lags where those methods operate (panel b). AvoKV-E addresses both by combining a payload-aware score with delayed observation. 16
