## Abstract 

Online agents can improve future service by constructing reusable tools, guidance, or model states, but this work competes with current requests for the same GPUs. Exploit-ing idle compute for self-evolution faces a fundamental systems constraint: benefits arrive only after an artifact is published and used, while pausing evolution leaves service capacity waiting for memory release and runtime recovery. An investment worth completing may therefore be worth postponing. We present LearnSched, a state-aware sched-uler that brings the reuse window of a capability and the timely return of compute into a common investment model. LearnSched incorporates candidate progress, checkpoint overhead, and the current recovery path into action val-ues. Under the same information and capacity constraints, it uses one-step counterfactual rollout to choose progress, checkpointing, or waiting relative to a fully costed window policy. We characterize handoff costs through independent A100 component measurements and evaluate action selec-tion in 1920 paired finite-model scenarios. When cold re-covery is expensive, waiting for longer execution windows can improve net value by avoiding handoffs; in evaluated warm-recovery scenarios, the strong window policy already achieves the same value. Retaining recoverable state can shorten capacity return and reduce the need for complex evolution scheduling. 

Keywords: online agents, self-evolution, resource schedul-ing, compute investment, capacity readiness, preemption 

## 1 Introduction 

An online coding agent repeatedly fails on the same class of requests. It can continue invoking a model for each request, or use prior trajectories to construct a reusable tool or guid-ance, validate it, and publish it for later use. Persistent de-mand can repay this investment through fewer model calls and better answers; short-lived demand may leave even a qualified artifact unused. When service and evolution share GPUs, construction also consumes compute and memory needed by current requests. The system must decide: should the next unit of compute serve now or improve later? 

Figure 1. Serving and self-evolution compete for shared GPU capacity. LoRA adaptation illustrates the tradeoff: in-vest in future capability while returning compute in time to meet current request deadlines. Figure 1 illustrates this choice through LoRA adaptation: improving the model incurs training and recovery costs be-fore future requests can benefit. This decision couples two timescales. Foreground re-quests consume resources under latency deadlines, while capability construction incurs costs now for benefits spread across future requests. A useful artifact can reduce service work, but only after qualification, publication, and actual use. Its benefit cannot compensate for requests that have already missed their deadlines. The scheduling problem therefore involves both the value of the investment and the service capacity available along its execution path. Running evolution during idle periods and pausing it at the next burst is a natural starting point. Yet an idle inter-val has two distinct boundaries: the latest start that leaves enough reuse to amortize construction, and the latest re-turn that restores capacity before service needs it. A pause command may leave device memory occupied; releasing memory may still leave a serving runtime to reconstruct. 

Stopping evolution, reclaiming memory, and making capac-ity ready to serve are distinct system events. GPU activity excludes CPU costs. Runtime state connects these boundaries. Termination discards unsaved progress, checkpointing consumes time, and retaining service state trades memory for faster recov-ery. Consider a short idle window followed by one long enough to complete construction. Immediate progress can publish the artifact slightly earlier but incur an extra check-point and cold recovery. Waiting pays when the avoided handoff costs exceed the benefits lost to delay. Cheap warm recovery can reverse this choice without changing the can-didate or future demand. Thus, an investment worth complet-ing need not be worth starting now. 

Expected future benefits have long guided adaptive com-pilation [2], inference and retraining allocation [4, 11], and agent learning during idle windows [21]. Evolution con-trol planes also incorporate cost signals [24]. We study the interaction between this investment perspective and the runtime mechanisms that preserve progress and restore ser-vice. With the same candidate, information, and execution mechanisms, when do durable progress and recovery costs change the best execution plan? Answering this question requires separating gains from better accounting, faster recovery, and different scheduling actions. We present LearnSched, a state-aware scheduler that compares remaining benefits and execution costs under a capacity-return constraint. Its model connects the useful lifetime of a capability to completed and durable progress, current service state, and the recovery path. These quan-tities determine whether to advance, checkpoint and re-turn, discard unsaved work, or wait. Execution components expose work pressure, publication and use, and capacity readiness to account for costs and benefits as they occur. LearnSched uses classical one-step rollout [3] relative to Costed Window, a reference that fully prices a greedy com-pletion plan. Both receive the same windows and recovery mechanisms. Comparing alternative continuations allows LearnSched to skip a costly short window or change execu-tion segments when this improves value; retaining the refer-ence action guarantees no loss in the accurate finite model. This formulation makes the added value of scheduling mea-surable against an already cost-aware reference. Our evaluation separates independent A100 component measurements and capability ledgers from paired finite-model scheduling. Across 1920 scenarios, full cost account-ing explains most of the benefit. Waiting adds value when cold recovery is expensive and longer windows lie ahead; all evaluated warm-recovery scenarios tie with Costed Win-dow. The resulting systems implication is that state reten-tion changes both return latency and the amount of value available to more complex planning. Runtime memory and investment policies thus require joint evaluation. Our contributions are: 

• A model connecting capability break-even condi-tions, durable progress, and state-dependent capacity return to explain when waiting improves net value. 

• A scheduler that compares progress and waiting un-der common constraints, with finite-model policy im-provement and an explicit scoring-error condition. 

• Component measurements, capability ledgers, and paired evaluation isolating accounting and schedul-ing gains and their dependence on recovery state. 2 Why Timing and State Matter 

Figure 2 examines three constraints on using shared GPUs for evolution. Positive expected return and available idle time alone do not determine the execution plan. 

Capacity must be available when requests need it. 

Raising the static background share from 10% to 25% in-creases bad requests across all four systems; at 50%, mean rates reach 37.88–84.48% (Figure 2(a)). An average resource budget hides the periods when foreground demand and background execution overlap. A profitable capability can therefore degrade service before its benefits arrive. The scheduler needs to control the timing of resource use as well as its volume: capacity borrowed under low load must be returned before the next service deadline, and future work savings cannot be credited before the artifact is used. 

Runtime state changes the usable window. Cold ser-vice readiness takes 69.99 s on average, whereas same-process warm recovery takes 0.487 s (Figure 2(b)). These paths leave substantially different amounts of an idle win-dow for useful evolution work. Segmentation compounds the difference: each return can incur recovery, and inter-rupted construction may require checkpointing or recom-putation. Two plans that complete the same amount of work can consequently have different total costs and publication times. Warm recovery also raises host proportional set size (PSS) from 4.77 to 11.16 GiB and retains 3.74 GiB on the de-vice. Keeping state buys faster return by occupying memory that other work could use. Plan comparisons must jointly price recovery latency, durable progress, and residency; a fixed pause overhead cannot capture their tradeoff. 

The constrained resource determines the cost of reuse. 

Under sustained tool demand, token-based amortization re-duces token cost to 0.2624 times that of no evolution but raises GPU active time to 1.8623 times (Figure 2(c)). Con-struction and the service calls it replaces have different de-vice costs per token. Counting avoided calls or tokens can therefore reverse the resource-cost ranking. This observa-tion is a cost comparison; quality gains and CPU execution still matter to application utility. Likewise, publishing more artifacts creates value only when subsequent requests use them. Investment accounting must connect construction to realized use and price both in the constrained resource. These observations motivate a joint decision over a can-didate, an execution interval, and a return path. Starting im-mediately captures earlier benefits, while waiting can avoid checkpointing and repeated recovery. In the paired finite-model example of Section 6.1, delaying publication by 4.952 s saves 70.104 s-eq. in execution and handoff costs under the same candidate and calendar. The relevant comparison is be-tween complete feasible plans, including their return paths and the benefits forgone while waiting.

4.4 Observation and Admission 

Candidates record task class, remaining stage and cost, com-pletion time, and reuse opportunities. Tool admission uses historical statistics and current arrivals; guidance uses class shares and quality proxies calibrated on independent his-tory. Hidden target tests and future task identifiers do not enter estimates. The prototypes reevaluate value while syn-thesis is pending and check windows and capacity there-after. Issued calls are charged through completion; full ROI optimization at each validation stage is not implemented. Admission estimates work as 𝑤 = 𝐿 max + 0.0625𝑃 , using prompt length 𝑃 and maximum output length 𝐿 max . A stage runs only if it fits with in-flight work within budgets of 1536 for tools and 1024 for guidance. This maximum-output reser-vation can be conservative. A separate contention controller uses arrival-aware additive increase and multiplicative de-crease (AIMD) to adapt the background share. 

4.5 Handoff and Publication Accounting 

Pollable waits insert control checks into long sleeps. Whole-GPU handoff checkpoints LoRA, terminates training, con-firms device-memory release, and starts a serving replica. Training resumes from the final durable file published after termination, avoiding a checkpoint-publication race. An al-ternative retains the serving process and restores its device state on return, trading resident memory for faster recovery. Tools publish solve(x) after passing eight public inputs; each configuration starts with an empty library. Guidance generates base and additional answers, preferring a base an-swer that passes public tests. Each request freezes its visible artifacts before dispatch. Model-call accounting charges ac-tual service, extra generation, construction, and validation: 

𝐶 all = 𝐶 base + 𝐶 extra + 𝐶 build + 𝐶 validate . (14) Failed builds, unpublished candidates, and unused calls are charged; absent base calls are not. Unknown usage remains missing. CPU execution is timed separately; the handoff ledger records durable progress, unsaved work, readiness, and device and host memory. 

## 5 Experimental Setup 

Component experiments measure contention, execution costs, and effective reuse. Comparisons use the same capa-bilities and resources, except Cal.*, which receives the future arrival calendar. Additional ledgers and sensitivity details appear in the appendices. 5.1 Hardware and Workloads 

Contention experiments cross Granite-3B-A0.8B (MoE) and Qwen2.5-Coder-7B (Dense) with vLLM and SGLang on one A100-80GB. Arrivals follow 4 →40 →4 requests/s for 10 s per phase, with at most 64 output tokens. SLOs are 1.2, 0.62, 1.8, and 1.2 s for MoE on SGLang and vLLM, then Dense on SGLang and vLLM. Bad requests include deadline misses from scheduled arrival and failures: the 60 runs contain 28,805 requests, including two failures. Background steps count synthetic GPU work without capability calibration. Tool experiments use Qwen3.5-9B on SGLang, a 512-token output limit, zero temperature, and no radix cache. In each 24 s window, sustained demand has 48 requests per block, stopped demand retains the same 12-request prefix, and sparse demand has requests at seconds 6 and 18. Con-figurations rebuild tools, isolate public tests from hidden evaluation, and run CPU paths in isolated workers. For guidance, we dispatch 114 known tasks from Live-CodeBench [10] under low–high–low arrivals. Five policies and five blocks each give 5700 requests. Whole-GPU handoff uses two A100s, Granite 3B, and rank-8 LoRA. One GPU serves; the other alternates be-tween training and a replica. The vLLM trace has 100 s of low load, a 60 s burst at 120 requests/s, and 30 s of low load, with at most 128 output tokens and a two-second SLO. Re-active handoff starts at burst onset; advance-notice handoff starts 90 s earlier, with experiment-specified notice. SGLang recovery is measured independently. 

5.2 Software 

Inference uses vLLM and SGLang. Python control uses asyn-cio and aiohttp for asynchronous dispatch and cost/latency recording; tool validation and execution use subprocesses with resource limits. The finite-state simulator implements Window, Costed Window, rollout, and exact DP under com-mon transition and cost models. 

## 6 Evaluation 

We evaluate plan selection, resource admission, capacity re-turn, and investment payback. Each experiment compares policies under its stated information and cost model. 

6.1 Value of State-Aware Scheduling 

All policies share complete window information, transitions, costs, and return constraints; exact DP gives the optimum in the finite macro-action model. 

Scenarios and costs. The frozen grid crosses six calen-dars (80–480 s windows separated by 20 s), four work sizes (30/60/120/180 s), five benefit rates (0.1/0.25/0.5/1/2 s-eq./s), two benefit tails (120/480 s), cold/warm recovery, and four initial states: fresh, half-complete and unsaved, saved and active, or saved in service mode. This gives 1920 scenarios with a 5 s work quantum; Appendix D specifies the calen-dars. Cold/warm recovery uses three-run SGLang means of 69.988/0.487 s. Independent LoRA measurements over 20 runs give save/load costs of 68.075/47.748 ms for about 13.24 MB of state. Execution and recovery cost one s-eq. per second; warm recovery adds 6.383 GiB of host PSS priced at 0.01 s-eq. per GiB-second. These separate measurements are combined in the model; memory pricing is assumed, unmea-sured entry/release delays are zero, and resident-memory throughput effects are not fitted. 

Aggregate value and distribution. Figure 4(a) reports mean DP gaps of 40.122 for Window, 28.819 for static ROI, 5.369 for Costed Window, and 0.246 s-eq. for rollout. Roll-out raises mean net value from 465.547 to 470.670, gaining 5.123 s-eq. (1.10%): full cost accounting explains most of the benefit. It improves 91 scenarios, ties in 1829, and never re-gresses. All improvements involve cold recovery: 71 back-loaded and 20 alternating calendars (Figure 4(b)); all 960 warm scenarios tie. Rollout matches DP in 1907 scenarios, but the remaining 13 have a maximum gap of 124.836. Every policy meets return deadlines because they share the same action constraint. Never can incur negative value from re-turning initially active states. At a 1 s quantum in 480 fresh-candidate scenarios, rollout improves 27 and ties in 453; its mean DP gap is zero, versus 5.869 for Costed Window. 

Why waiting helps. The fresh case in Figure 4(c) has 30 s of work, benefit rate 0.25, a back-loaded calendar, horizon 1020 s, and cold recovery. Costed Window advances 5 s at 200, checkpoints, and finishes with 25 s at 300; it publishes at 325.048 s for cost 170.092. Rollout starts at 300 and publishes at 330 for cost 99.988. Waiting saves 70.104 s-eq., loses 1.238 in benefits, and gains 68.866 net. With warm recovery, both publish at 30 s in the first window. 

6.2 Resource Allocation and Admission 

Adaptive shares. Among static shares of 0, 10%, 25%, and 50%, only shares up to 10% satisfy the 1% bad-request limit in every repeat across four systems. ## 7 Discussion and Related Work 

7.1 State Management and Scheduling Complexity 

State management changes both feasible windows and plan values. With expensive cold recovery, checkpointing and segmented execution can incur substantial handoff costs, making a later, longer window more valuable. Warm recov-ery can bring a window policy close to the value of more complex planning. Runtime design and scheduling there-fore interact: retaining state must be valued through faster return, less repeated work, and residency costs. Planning and prediction should focus where they change actions.

7.3 Related Work 

Jalapeño selects recompilation based on expected future ex-ecution benefits [2]; computation selection and metareason-ing study when to invest further computation [5, 9]. We in-clude qualification, actual use, and service-recovery paths in remaining investment value, and use classical rollout [3] to compare capacity-constrained execution plans. Ekya coordinates inference and retraining, RECL reuses historical models, and LeMix jointly schedules LLM training and inference [4, 11, 14]. MetaClaw learns during inactiv-ity and calendar windows [21]. Yan et al. propose a cost-aware evolution control plane with multiple actions and instantiate online weight updates through AReaL2.0 [24]. LearnSched focuses on break-even value, durable progress, and capacity-return timing for discrete capabilities, eval-uating plan differences under the same information and execution method. Gandiva time-slices and migrates training jobs, Tire-sias targets job completion time, and AntMan dynamically shares spare GPU resources [6, 22, 23]. Salus provides fast job switching and memory sharing, while REEF supports microsecond-scale preemption for DNN inference [8, 26]; these mechanisms inform the cost of lending and returning capacity. Clockwork uses predictable execution to meet in-ference latency targets [7]. Orca schedules at iteration gran-ularity, Sarathi-Serve uses chunked prefills, and DistServe separates prefill and decoding to improve LLM serving un-der latency constraints [1, 25, 30]. PagedAttention and SGLang improve execution effi-ciency [12, 29], while Reflexion and Self-Refine provide feedback-based improvement mechanisms [15, 17]. Voy-ager accumulates reusable executable skills [18]. Recent ad-vances extend these mechanisms to evolutionary program optimization and self-directed model adaptation [16, 31], as well as skill–tool co-evolution and adaptive workflow evolution [19, 20]. DGM and Hyperagents investigate agent self-modification, with the latter also making the meta-level improvement procedure itself editable [27, 28]. These ad-vances expand both the capabilities available for reuse and the mechanisms that construct them. Investment schedul-ing must therefore re-estimate construction and validation costs, expected reuse benefits, and capacity-return overhead as the system evolves. 

## 8 Conclusion 

LearnSched treats online evolution as a compute investment with delayed returns and state-dependent switching costs. Counterfactual rollout compares progress and waiting un-der capacity-return constraints. Measurements and finite-model results show that waiting can avoid costly cold hand-offs, while warm recovery reduces this advantage. Effective evolution scheduling must jointly account for future bene-fits and timely capacity return. References

D.2 Transition Costs and Exact Evaluation 

Cold and warm recovery take 69.988 and 0.487 s, using three-run SGLang means. Save and load take 68.075 and 47.748 ms from an independent LoRA microbenchmark. These de-lays remain exact wall-clock costs even though progress ad-vances in discrete quanta. Execution and recovery cost one s-eq. per second. Warm recovery additionally prices 6.383 GiB of incremental host PSS at 0.01 s-eq. per GiB-second, or 0.06383 s-eq. per second of charged residency. The measurements come from separate experiments. The memory price is assumed, unmeasured entry and release delays are zero, and resident-device-memory effects on throughput are not fitted. A residency bill depends on re-tained memory and retention time; recovery latency alone cannot determine net value. At window 𝑘 , a feasible macro-action must satisfy Equa-tion (10), including loading, checkpointing when selected, and the return path.
