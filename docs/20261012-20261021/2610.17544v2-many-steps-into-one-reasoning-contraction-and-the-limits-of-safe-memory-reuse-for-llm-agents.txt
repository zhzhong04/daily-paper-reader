# Many Steps into One: Reasoning Contraction and the Limits of Safe Memory Reuse for LLM Agents 

Jianghong Zhou Hao Rong Chen Lin Daoan Zhang Abstract 

Thought templates, skill libraries, agent memo-ries and caches turn many reasoning steps into one. We call this reasoning contraction . Acontracted memory saves reasoning steps and errors until its facts change, then quietly an-swers wrong. Most systems trust memories by counting successes and failures. We prove that such counters are badly tuned change detectors: a symmetric counter notices a partial change only after a power of its false-alarm horizon, and an unclipped one may never notice it. On five LLMs answering multi-hop questions over edited facts, ExpeL- and ACE-style counters serve 7.0–13 .5× more stale answers than certi-fied memories given the same information, and on code tasks with an exact verifier 35 –73 ×

more. Safety also has a price. When facts change and each memory is judged only by its own verified outcomes, one number, the cer-tification load , sets how much reasoning any safe memory can skip, and under Zipf demand it yields a scaling law. Our recipe weighs each memory’s failures by its own likelihood ratio, certifies its utility against live reasoning, and restarts the certificate when facts change, with the likelihood-ratio detector as the safety net for unannounced changes; for large edits it also invalidates the memory. 

1 Introduction 

Everyone builds reasoning memories. Thought templates (Yang et al., 2024a), reasoning and workflow memories (Ouyang et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2025b; Suzgun et al., 2026), behavior hand-books (Didolkar et al., 2025), skill libraries (Wang et al., 2024a, 2025a), answer caches (Bang, 2023; Schroeder et al., 2026), and the memories that agents share (Zhang et al., 2025a) or rewrite them-selves from (Zhang et al., 2026a) all do one thing: they compile a derivation of m ≥ 2 steps into a one-step contracted memory for later instances of the problem. We call this reasoning contraction .Trusting such a memory is a sequential test, bound by the limits of change detection and certification. 

Live reasoning is costly and fragile. LLM rea-soners buy accuracy with computation: voting over sampled chains (Wang et al., 2023), tree search (Yao et al., 2023a), tool calls (Yao et al., 2023b) and self-critique (Shinn et al., 2023; Madaan et al., 2023). Every step is another chance to err. On the unedited facts of our multi-hop benchmark, Qwen3-8B’s live accuracy falls from 0.63 at two hops to 

0.31 at three and 0.19 at four (Appendix D). A veri-fied contracted memory answers in one step; on our edited streams it raises Qwen3-8B’s accuracy from 

0.379 to 0.648 with fewer calls (Table 3). The price is a new failure mode shared by all these systems: when the facts behind a memory change, it keeps answering, wrongly. This is the utility problem of macro learning (Minton, 1990), now for LLM memories over changing facts. 

Memories are trusted by counters, which are badly tuned change detectors. ExpeL and ACE vote memories up and down (Zhao et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2026b), and validation-gated mem-ories clip a Hebbian strength (Singh and Yu, 2025). Every clipped counter is a cumulative-sum (CUSUM) change detector in disguise, testing an 

implied change that nobody chose (Section 3). A symmetric counter assumes that a stale memory is as often wrong as a fresh one was right, so count-ing votes equally is provably the wrong detector for partial staleness: when an edit leaves a mem-ory failing less than half the time, the counter’s evidence drifts the wrong way, and its delay grows as a power of its false-alarm horizon, not its loga-rithm (Theorem 1, Figure 1b). Unclipped coun-ters are worse: credit banked while the mem-ory was healthy must be spent before retirement. Safety has a price. Can a memory be safe and still skip most reasoning? One number decides: the 

certification load Θ = ν ln(1 /δ )/(pKL) , which combines the rate of change ( ν), the risk tolerated (δ), the audit rate ( p) and how much one verified outcome reveals ( KL ). Whatever its algorithm, ev-ery safe memory must, in the worst case and as 

δ → 0, leave a share Fπ(Θ) of queries to multi-step reasoning, and a restarted test achieves it (The-orem 3, Figure 1c). Under Zipf demand it scales as κsΘ1−1/s (Corollary 4): popular problems can be contracted; rare ones change before they can be trusted. 

The recipe. Three changes turn a counter into a certificate , a rule that lets a memory answer in one step only while the risk of trusting a bad one stays below δ (Section 5). (i) Weigh each memory’s failures against its successes by its own likelihood ratio, set by its healthy and stale fail-ure rates; on KQA-S TREAM this detects staleness in 1.3–5.9× fewer uses than the best tested fixed weighting. (ii) Certify utility, not accuracy, by bet-ting on the paired difference with live reasoning on the same query, with no estimate of live accu-racy. (iii) Restart a memory’s certificate whenever its answers or the live reasoning it competes with change, so every trust decision rests on current evi-dence. Where edits are substantial, our deployment also invalidates memories an edit announces, and the likelihood-ratio detector is the safety net for unannounced edits; it cuts stale answers 9–12 ×

against invalidation alone. On five models it trusts under one below-break-even memory per stream (with notices; counters: 111 –595 ), and, scoring an-swers alone, makes no false certification at δ ≤ 0.1.2Agent memories and skill libraries with a verifier should therefore adopt these changes, and memory benchmarks should edit facts.

2 Contraction as a memory lifecycle 

Queries, templates, macros. A reasoner serves a stream of queries. Each query instantiates a tem-plate i, a canonical derivation skeleton such as a relation path in a knowledge base, a lemma chain, or a tool-call sequence. Template i is drawn with probability πi and has a live derivation of mi ≥ 2

steps (LLM calls). A macro for template i is a one-step operator formed from a verified deriva-tion: a materialized composed relation, a stored lemma, or a distilled direct-answer note. Its relia-bility r is the probability that it answers a random instance of i correctly. On each query the system answers live (cost m, correct with probability a), 

folded (cost 1, answer from the trusted macro), or in 

shadow mode (live answer, macro evaluated along-side). A verifier (exact match, execution, or a unit test) labels the macro’s answer on audited queries, a fraction p ∈ (0 , 1] of the template’s shadow or folded queries. With error price L (in steps), a folded query costs 1 + L(1 − r) and a live one 

m + L(1 − a), so folding pays if and only if 

r > r ⋆ := a − (m − 1) /L. (1) 

The lifecycle. Contraction is a memory lifecycle (Figure 1a): form a candidate from a verified trace, 

certify it in shadow mode, fold it, monitor it, retire 

it when it goes stale, and re-form . Two decisions carry all the risk: when may a candidate be trusted 

(certification) and when has a trusted macro gone stale (retirement). Verifier outcomes Y ∈ { 0, 1} of a macro are conditionally Bernoulli given the past, with mean equal to its current reliability; this allows adaptive usage and drift. A macro is stale once an edit has raised its failure rate: healthy macros fail with probability q0, stale ones with q1 > q 0.

Maintenance rules in current systems. ExpeL gives a new insight importance 2, adds 1 per up-vote or edit, subtracts 1 per down-vote and deletes it at 0 (Zhao et al., 2024). ACE keeps unbounded helpful/harmful counts, and its released code flags a bullet when harmful ≥ helpful and harmful > 0

(Zhang et al., 2026b); our ACE-style baseline treats a flag as retirement. We idealize their LLM-issued votes as one vote per verified use. Validation-gated Hebbian memories clip a strength to [0 , 1] with time decay (Singh and Yu, 2025); we analyze the symmetric variant c ← min {1, c + η+} on success and c ← c − η− on failure, retiring below τ .

8 Conclusion 

Reusing a reasoning memory safely is a sequential test, and vote counters (ExpeL, ACE) are change detectors tuned to the wrong change. Three rules follow. (1) Weigh a memory’s failures by its own likelihood ratio: at failure rate 0.05 → 0.25 a fail-ure weighs about 7 successes, not 1. (2) Certify utility against live reasoning; the load Θ prices skipped reasoning in the worst case. (3) Restart at every notice ( 0 false certifications in 5,779 at-risk epochs at δ = 0 .05 , answers scored alone) and, for substantial edits, invalidate too, with the detector as safety net ( 9–12 × fewer stale answers when no-8tices are missed). Memory benchmarks should edit facts and count stale answers. 

Limitations 

Scope. Our results concern memories whose an-swers an exact verifier checks, such as knowledge-base execution or unit tests. The evidence comes from three domains and five open models of up to 8B parameters: multi-hop reasoning over a knowl-edge graph with controlled fact edits, a natural time-line of Wikidata temporal qualifiers (four models; Appendix D.4.4), and EvalPlus code tasks, whose memories go stale under requirement revisions and re-enacted Python API changes (Appendix D.6). Each instance–version is decoded once; seed vari-ance is measured on a subset (Appendix D.4.3). The theorems assume that each audit reveals one verified outcome whose law is set by the mem-ory’s current reliability, and that facts change at random times; Appendices A to C state the ex-act conditions and Appendix D the replay setting. Per-epoch safety needs change notices that cover every change of the certificate’s paired-utility null, including changes of live reasoning; our notices cover answer changes (Appendix D.5.1). Some change information is necessary on every template whose epochs contain an audit with probability above δ/r 0 (Proposition 24); without an edit log, restarts triggered by the memory’s own detector keep every decision that rests on post-change evi-dence safe (Proposition 25). 

Future work. Five directions extend this scope. (i) A formation-aware limit: on real streams, cov-erage also depends on how often the live agent produces a verified trace from which a candidate can form, and a limit that models formation would predict real-system coverage. (ii) Noisy and LLM-judge verifiers, which would bring text memories into reach; the certification load already prices noise through the observed channel, and a certifi-cate built for noisy paired labels is the next step (Appendix D.2.3). (iii) Longer and denser natural edit histories, such as full knowledge-base revision logs, where change notices arrive with their own delays and errors, and real library drift on bench-marks with version-specific APIs, where staleness reaches more programs than re-enacted depreca-tions do. (iv) Larger models. (v) Open problems in the theory: a square-root law for adaptive audit budgets, a joint detection–certification limit that prices stale answers, the exact optimal certifier for one template, near-optimal retirement for an un-known post-change rate by parallel or GLR rules, and the agreement-aware version of the limit. 

Ethical considerations 

This work analyzes when stored reasoning may be trusted, and its main practical implication is safety-positive: it bounds how often a memory-augmented reasoner serves stale or spurious shortcuts, and it shows that common counter rules may never retire a stale memory. The datasets we use (KQA Pro, CC BY-SA 4.0; MuSiQue, CC BY 4.0; Wikidata, CC0; EvalPlus, Apache-2.0, with MBPP, CC BY 4.0, and HumanEval, MIT) are public and contain no personal data beyond public facts about notable en-tities; our synthetic knowledge-base edits, natural-edit timeline and code requirement revisions are released as edit logs for research (under CC BY-SA 4.0 where derived from KQA Pro; our code is MIT-licensed). All questions and tasks are in English or Python. All models are open-weight and were run locally without paid APIs. AI assistants were used to draft proofs, code and text and to run exper-iments, and independent AI referee agents checked them; the authors take responsibility for all con-tent. The guarantees hold only under the stated assumptions, and deploying certified memory in high-stakes settings still requires a verifier whose errors are understood.

C.2 Changes, carried-over evidence and the lifetime ledger 

Definition 1 counts every fold event of a bad can-didate in an epoch, including candidates formed in earlier epochs. We separate what is proved for the 

idealized certificate from what holds for the two implemented ledgers: without restarts and with restarts (the restart-only certificate of Section 5). 

Idealized. Changes are announced, all certifica-tion evidence is discarded at each change, and one test per template (SPRT, or the grid mixture of The-orem 17) restarts in each epoch at level δ; if several candidates are tested within an epoch, they share 

δ, e.g. through a per-epoch ledger. Every fold in an epoch then rests on that epoch’s evidence alone, Ville’s inequality gives Definition 1 for every fold event, and Theorem 3(ii) and Corollary 20 describe this certificate. 

Ledger with restarts (restart only). The replay notifies the policy of every edit that changes the gold answer of some instance routed to a template; epochs of a template are the intervals between such edits, within which the macro’s reliability is con-stant. Compute and software. All LLM runs used one NVIDIA RTX 4080 SUPER GPU (16 GB). Models were served with vLLM 0.30 (Kwon et al., 2023) (FlashAttention backend) and PyTorch 2.13 under Ubuntu 22.04. The 8B models were served with FP8 weights: Qwen3-8B-FP8 natively (Yang et al., 2025), and Granite-3.3-8B-Instruct (IBM Granite Team, 2025), Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 (Jiang et al., 2023) and OLMo-3-7B-Instruct (Team OLMo, 2025) with on-the-fly FP8 quantization. Qwen3-4B ran in BF16. The natural-edit timeline used 

4.75 GPU-hours and the code domain 17 .2 GPU-hours of wall time. Mistral has no system role, so its system message is prepended to the first user turn. Granite-3.3’s chat template embeds the cur-rent date, so its prompts change with the calendar day; all Granite prompts were rendered with the date pinned to 2026-09-25, and prompt hashes con-firm that this reproduces the main-run prompts ex-actly. Qwen3 “thinking” was disabled. Main-table decoding used T =0 .7, top-p 0.8, top-k 20 , with a deterministic per-request seed. Every LLM re-quest carries a content hash. Results are appended to checkpoint shards and skipped on restart, so all runs are resumable. Tables and figures are gener-ated by script from the raw logs. 

Mechanism computations. Run lengths of integer-score rules were computed by solving the Markov chain of the reflected walk. The gap of SPRT certification uses Lemma 10 with the exact sample-size distribution of the one-sided SPRT, obtained by dynamic programming on the lattice of log-likelihood-ratio values. Sums over templates run to 10 7 with rigorous tail brackets. Natural template streams. KQA Pro templates are the programs of the train and validation sets (106,173 questions) with entity names, strings, numbers and dates abstracted. Function, relation, attribute and concept names are kept, giving 45,544 templates. MuSiQue templates abstract each single-hop sub-question of the answerable training set (19,938 questions; 10,686 templates). We abstract the relation phrase after “ ≫” when it is present, and otherwise the question with bridge entities replaced by placeholders. Exponents were esti-mated from rank-frequency data (Sibuya-type es-timator on counts ≤10 ), from the Clauset–Shalizi– Newman tail fit, and from Heaps’ law. KQA-S TREAM construction. We index the KQA Pro knowledge base: 16,960 entities, 794 concepts, 363 relations (726 relation–direction types) and 415,702 directed edges. Our path ex-ecutor agrees with the official KoPL engine on 600 sampled one- and two-hop programs (2,187 steps). A template is an anchor concept plus a relation path of 2–4 hops. It is kept if at least 60 anchors have 1–3 answers of a consistent concept, the path does not immediately backtrack, and no single answer covers more than half of the anchors. From 2,250 32 qualifying distinct paths we selected 300 templates (120/120/60 with 2/3/4 hops), covering 17 anchor concepts, 51 answer concepts and 114 relations, and sampled 60 anchors each (18,000 instances). A random pattern verbalizes each instance question; 296 of the 300 templates have three LLM-written patterns with a single entity slot (the rest have fewer valid patterns). An edit event replaces the object of a relation for a fraction f ∈ { 0.25 , 0.6}

of the relevant subjects with a random entity of the same concept, keeping inverse edges consistent. Each of three sequences has 24 events. An event changes the gold answer of 223 ( f =0 .25 ) or 386 (f =0 .6) instances on average and touches 3.9% of templates, which become 32% or 59% stale respec-tively. Templates that share an edited relation go stale together. The per-template change rate grows with derivation length (events that hit a 2/3/4-hop template: 2.7/4.2/5.5% ; Spearman 0.34 ), which makes staleness itself a multi-step-reasoning phe-nomenon. 

LLM paths. The live agent answers hop by hop. At each step its prompt contains the question, the linked anchor, the path so far, the current frontier (up to 5 entity names) and a numbered list of the (relation, direction) pairs available at the frontier (up to 60, sorted by frequency). It outputs JSON that either takes one hop or answers, with at most 5 hops, and may filter the final frontier by one of its concepts. The answer is the resulting entity set, and it is correct if it equals the gold set of the current knowledge-base version. For Qwen3-8B a live query uses 3.24 calls with 1,486 prompt and 

62 completion tokens on average; on the unedited knowledge base its live accuracy is 0.625 , 0.305 

and 0.190 at 2, 3 and 4 hops ( 0.410 over all 18,000 instances). Routing , the one LLM step of a macro, retrieves the top-5 templates by BM25 over their verbalized patterns and asks the LLM for a tem-plate id or NONE. It runs against the full, fixed template catalog, so its outcome does not depend on the policy. Its accuracy is 0.939 for Qwen3-8B, and the correct template is among the top 5 for all but 40 of the 18,000 instances. The direct 

baselines use one call: a parametric answer with-out the knowledge base, or whole-path generation from the question and the BM25 top-60 relation directions, executed on the current version. For each call we log prompt and completion tokens and latency. After an edit, live traces are re-run only for instances whose earlier trace or gold path touches an edited edge; all other traces are reused unchanged, which is exact because the agent’s in-puts are unchanged. A brute-force re-run of all instances at versions 0, 8, 16 , 24 of every sequence matched in all 216 ,000 comparisons.

D.2.4 A vCache-style semantic cache Re-implementation. We re-implemented vCache from Algorithms 1–2 of its paper (Schroeder et al., 2026); the following details are our design choices. A query is looked up against the nearest cached question by cosine similarity (ties go to the newest entry). Each entry fits a sigmoid of similarity by ridge-penalized logistic maximum likelihood ( λ = 10 −3), with a delta-method upper confidence bound on its threshold. The exploration probability is the minimum of the paper’s Eq. 11 over a grid of 49 values of its error parameter, and 1 while an entry has no observations. An exploration answers live, evaluates the cached answer in shadow at no cost, and adds the query to the cache only if the cached answer was judged wrong; an exploitation returns the cached answer at no LLM call, and embedding compute is not charged. Labels are vCache’s own (agree : cached answer equals the live answer) or gold correctness on audited queries ( audit ). There is no retirement. The plug-in variant uses the point estimate without the confidence band, which is optimistic. The vCache paper used GTE-large, E5-large-v2 and OpenAI embeddings. None was available offline, so we use all-MiniLM-L6-v2 (384 dimensions), with TF-IDF as a sensitivity check; the median nearest-neighbour similarity over the 17,927 unique questions is 0.81 (MiniLM) and 0.66 (TF-IDF). A faithful replication would need a 1024-dimensional embedding and the authors’ code to confirm the confidence band. A cache hit on a different question counts as stale if the cached answer would have been correct for that query when the entry was formed.

D.3.3 Per-epoch safety 

A template epoch is a run, a routed template and an interval between edits that change the gold answer of some instance routed to that template. A false fold is a fold of a candidate whose true reliability (over all instances routed to the template, at stream popularity) is below r⋆ at the fold version; this is the SpF event of Table 2. An epoch is at risk if a candidate that is below break-even at some version of the epoch is under test in it, and the per-epoch false-fold frequency is the share of at-risk epochs with at least one false fold. A false fold is carried over if the candidate formed in an earlier epoch. D.6 A code domain with an exact verifier: CodeStream 

CodeStream tests the theory on a second real do-main, chosen so that the verifier is exact and mem-47 ories are what tool-building agents store: a veri-fied program, reused as a tool (Wang et al., 2024a; Cai et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024c; Yuan et al., 2024). It writes the same potential-outcome tables as KQA-S TREAM , so every policy, metric and re-play setting of Appendix D runs on it unchanged; only the domain, the stream length and the cost unit differ. The setting was fixed after a two-model pilot, before the full run. 

Tasks, queries and the verifier. We use 521 EvalPlus tasks (Liu et al., 2023): 359 from MBPP+ (v0.2.0; Austin et al., 2021) and 162 from Hu-manEval+ (v0.1.10; Chen et al., 2021). We ex-clude 21 tasks: 17 with a special oracle or a failing canonical solution, and 4 with fewer than 10 usable hidden inputs. A query is a task plus one hidden plus-test input, 20 per task ( 10 ,420 queries); its answer is the function’s output. The verifier is EvalPlus’s own check against the reference output of the task version in force: exact equality, or close-ness within the task’s tolerance for floating-point outputs. Each (program, version, input set) runs once and is cached, so every policy sees the same verdicts; re-executing the 6,330 sandbox runs of the table build in fresh processes changes 57 of 

126 ,600 verdicts ( 0.05% ), from programs whose output is not deterministic (e.g. set order). 

Live reasoning and memories. The live rea-soner is a solve–test–repair agent. It writes the function from the task prompt (temperature 0.7,at most 768 new tokens; Qwen3 with thinking dis-abled), runs it on the visible tests (the EvalPlus base inputs) in a sandbox of the current version, and, if a test fails, gets the failing call, the expected output and the output or exception it produced. It makes at most three attempts; the final program is run on the query input. Each task has 20 samples, one per query, so live outcomes of different queries are in-dependent samples. A memory is the final program of a live trace whose answer was audited correct; folded, it is executed directly on later queries of the same task, in the current environment. Memories are thus compiled plans in the sense of Section 6: an environment change can break them, and a re-quirement revision changes the answers they are checked against. Some programs pass the visible tests but fail other hidden inputs, EvalPlus’s own finding that base tests under-specify tasks; they are the domain’s candidates that are bad at formation, so certification has real work to do. 

Edit catalogue. Each timeline has 14 edits (15 versions) at uniform stream times, in the order A1, S1, A2, A3, S2, A4, S3, A5, A6, S4, A7, S5, A8, S6. Requirement revisions S1–S6 are the main source of staleness. Each batch revises 22 tasks ( 6% of the revisable tasks; 132 per timeline, each at most once) by appending one natural post-processing change to the prompt as “Requirement update: . . . ”: sort, deduplicate, lowercase, reduce modulo 10 9+7 , clamp negatives to 0, round to two decimals, return ’Yes’ /’No’ , or return a list instead of a tuple. The reference output changes exactly on the inputs whose output the change affects, so staleness is partial or total by construction from the real input distribution (in the design-time catalogue the share of a revised task’s queries whose answer changes ranged from 0.05 to 1, median about 0.6). The three timelines (seeds 101, 202, 303) revise different tasks. API edits A1–A8 re-enact docu-mented Python breaking changes in the sandbox (Table 20); they change no reference output, and a memory goes stale only if its own program changes behavior. Error messages carry the migration hint, as real deprecations do, so live reasoning can repair from test feedback.

Ties: the certification load on a real domain. 

The cost unit changes how much certified reuse the stream allows, and Theorem 6(ii) says why. Audited memory–live pairs of the certified poli-cies agree on the outcome in 94 –97% of cases ( 84 –

99% per model), and live reasoning re-derives the memory’s exact program in 49 –59% (21 –79% per model; 21 –33% for Mistral-7B and OLMo-3-7B). In call accounting, a tie in which live reasoning succeeds at its first attempt has paired utility ex-actly 0; this holds for 84 –86% of audited pairs (67 –89% per model). The utility law then has a large atom at zero, so KL inf (PZ ) is small and cer-tification is slow, and one-step coverage drops to 

0.32 , against 0.61 in step accounting, where every tie is positive evidence because live reasoning costs at least one step more than the memory. For ours, the drop tracks the share of zero-utility pairs across models: Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-4B ( 88 –89% ) keep 

34 –36% of their step-accounting coverage, Granite-3.3-8B and OLMo-3-7B ( 86 –87% ) 52 –53% , and Mistral-7B ( 68% ) 81% . This is the certification load Θ ∝ 1/(pKL inf ) of Sections 4 and 5 observed on a real domain: an exact verifier and a strong live reasoner make audits nearly uninformative, and safe contraction must wait for the queries on which the two differ. 

Scope. API edits break few programs: in the pi-lot, A1 breaks 3.7–4.9% of the distinct verified programs and the other seven edits together at most 

7 programs per model, because MBPP and Hu-manEval programs mostly use builtins. They test the unannounced channel and changelog notices, but we claim no staleness magnitudes for real li-brary drift; a benchmark with version-specific APIs would measure them. Requirement revisions are controlled changes of real tasks, as the fact edits of KQA-S TREAM are.

E.4 Why rare templates stay uncovered 

Six templates of one stream show the mechanism of Theorem 3: a template is covered only if its epoch outlasts n⋆i /(pπ i). We use edit sequence 101 and seed 0, with SPRT certification. T048, T027, T110 and T115 have matched difficulty: live solves 59 of 60 instances, the gold-path macro has r = 1 .000 ,and n⋆i is 208–213. Only their popularity ranks differ (Table 26). The head certifies within 38 labels; the tail never does. T110 forms at t = 5 ,301 from a correct trace for “What record labels are connected to grad-uates of Radcliffe College?”. It gets 8 of the 39 labels it needs before edit v7 lowers its reliability to 0.733 < r ⋆ = 0 .952 . T115 and T014 get 5 and 2 labels in the whole stream. In the 30 main-setting streams, the SPRT needs a median of 39 labels per fold (all 1,201 folds), fewer than the first-order n⋆i (208–213 for the tem-plates above), which assumes the least favorable candidate. Ranks 101–300 get 7.5–14.5 audits per template-stream on average, and none of their 5,771 template-streams was ever folded. 

Takeaway. With a fixed audit budget, templates rarer than the load are not certified between changes; coverage comes from popular templates. 

F Extended related work 

Reasoning memory and skill libraries. LLM systems increasingly store reusable reasoning: thought templates (Yang et al., 2024a), strategies and insights (Suzgun et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2024; Ouyang et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026b; Didolkar et al., 2025), workflows and program-matic skills (Wang et al., 2025b,a, 2024a,c), veri-fied lemmas (Wang et al., 2024b), and long-term memories (Zhong et al., 2024; Park et al., 2023; Chhikara et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025). Many never retire an item (Yang et al., 2024a; Ouyang et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2025b). The rest use hand-set rules. ExpeL (Zhao et al., 2024) keeps an integer importance count that starts at 2, moves by ±1 on up- and down-votes and deletes the item at 0. ACE (Zhang et al., 2026b) keeps un-bounded helpful/harmful tallies. Other rules in-clude clipped Hebbian strengths with time decay (Singh and Yu, 2025; Zhu et al., 2026), lifetime failure rates thresholded at 1/2 (Chen et al., 2026), EWMA utilities (Zhang et al., 2026c), Beta counts (Forouzandeh et al., 2026), usage-frequency prun-ing (Wang et al., 2024c), time-to-live (Li et al., 2025), sliding-window utilities (Pu et al., 2026) and paired with/without-memory audits (Zhang et al., 2026e; Gao et al., 2026). MQuAKE (Zhong et al., 2023), StreamingQA and RealTime QA (Liška et al., 2022; Kasai et al., 2023) evaluate a model after knowledge edits; KQA-S TREAM evaluates a memory lifecycle over a stream with recurring templates and interleaved edits. The statistical anal-yses we know of either prove stationary consis-tency of a two-counter “memory worth” (Simsek, 2026) or prove that an EMA converges (Zhang et al., 2026c). Sun et al. (2025) recast memory management in decision-theoretic terms but give no tests. Asadolahi et al. (2026) show that self-graded memory scores are inflated unless verifier errors are independent. Sequential tests enter LLM pipelines only to audit trajectories (Sadhuka et al., 2025) or to stop sampling within one query (Lee et al., 2025). We show that these maintenance rules are change detectors: clipped counters are Page CUSUMs with mis-specified scores, and unclipped tallies have no renewal. We quantify their detection delay and replace them with certified folding and likelihood-ratio retirement. 

55 Step skipping versus system-level contraction. 

Model-level methods train an LLM to skip or inter-nalize steps (Liu et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2024; Hao et al., 2025), to compress chains of thought (Xia et al., 2025), or to decide when to think (Zhang et al., 2025b); see Sui et al. (2025) for a survey. These methods change what one forward pass can compute. They choose training data offline. At in-ference time nothing decides whether a template’s one-step answer can be trusted, or notices when it has gone stale. The problem is real. Latent multi-hop composition is unreliable (Yang et al., 2024b), and consensus-based early exit is unsafe (Mo et al., 2026). Contraction is complementary. It treats any distilled routine as a macro and decides per template, over time and with explicit error control, when to serve that macro and when to retire it. 

Caching, routing and cascades with guaran-tees. Semantic caches reuse responses for similar prompts (Bang, 2023), and agent systems cache plan templates (Zhang et al., 2025c). Caching theory gives optimal content under stationary de-mand (Zhu et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2026). Cas-cades and routers decide per query (Chen et al., 2024a; Aggarwal et al., 2024). Where these sys-tems carry guarantees, the guarantees are fixed-sample risk control under exchangeability. Exam-ples are CALM (Schuster et al., 2022), which uses Learn-then-Test (Angelopoulos et al., 2025), PAC and group-conditional reasoning (Zeng et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2026), and conformal early stopping (Wang et al., 2026). Two works are closer. • B-PAC (Yu et al., 2026b) certifies a global thinking/non-thinking router anytime, with inverse-propensity-weighted test supermartin-gales. Our certificate uses the same tools (betting, inverse-propensity weights, Ville’s inequality); what differs is the object: one certificate per template, restarted at changes and followed by retirement, over a memory’s whole lifecycle. • vCache (Schroeder et al., 2026) bounds the per-request error of cache hits using random-ized LLM verification. Its bound assumes i.i.d. prompts and a correct similarity model, and exploration can stop entirely. Staleness has been studied only empirically (Man-soor et al., 2026; Wu and Canedo, 2026), although model behavior does drift (Chen et al., 2024b). Anytime-valid monitors watch whole models, not reusable items (Podkopaev and Ramdas, 2022; Richter et al., 2025; Khosravi and Huo, 2026). We certify a reusable derivation with a test that stays valid at all times, under adaptive shadowing and arbitrary drift below the null. The indifference zone comes from the loss. Certification is followed by quickest-detection retirement, and the result yields population-level coverage laws. 

Sequential testing and change detection. Certi-fied folding rests on the SPRT (Wald, 1945; Wald and Wolfowitz, 1948), Ville’s inequality (Ville, 1939), and testing by betting (Shafer, 2021; Ram-das et al., 2023; Howard et al., 2021; Waudby-Smith and Ramdas, 2024). Retirement rests on Page’s CUSUM (Page, 1954) and its optimality (Lorden, 1971; Moustakides, 1986; Lai, 1998). Two of our results are instances of older work. The counter identity matches cumulative-score and in-teger CUSUM charts (Munford, 1980; Brook and Evans, 1972). Our likelihood-ratio learning rates are the Bernoulli CUSUM (Reynolds and Stoum-bos, 1999). Analyses of mis-specified CUSUM as-sume positive post-change drift (Molloy and Ford, 2017; Cooper and Meyn, 2024), or note only quali-tatively that delay can exceed the false-alarm time (Molloy, 2021). Wu and Yang (2016, Thm. 2) state that the delay is infinite in the negative-drift regime; their proof exchanges an expecta-tion and a minimum over the CUSUM’s restarts. Observation-controlled detection (Banerjee and Veeravalli, 2013; Xu et al., 2021) and e-detectors (Shin et al., 2024) are the natural comparisons for audited and composite settings. Using Cramér– Lundberg asymptotics (Iglehart, 1972; Siegmund, 1985; Asmussen, 2003), we make explicit the full delay–ARL phase diagram for additive-score detec-tors. It includes the power law D ≍ Aθ1/θ 0 under negative drift, where Wu and Yang (2016) state an infinite delay; Appendix A locates the step of their proof that fails and gives a counterexample. 

Occupancy, freshness and scaling laws. Our static amortization law is a corollary of occupancy asymptotics (Karlin, 1967; Gnedin et al., 2007). Its constant follows from rare-mass results (Ohannes-sian and Dahleh, 2012), and Good–Turing theory estimates and forecasts it (Good, 1953; Good and Toulmin, 1956; McAllester and Schapire, 2000; Orlitsky et al., 2016). For n0 = 1 it is the cold-start or characteristic-time miss ratio of caching (Fagin, 1977; Che et al., 2002; Jelenkovi´ c, 1999). 56 Admission after k hits generalizes this case (Mar-tina et al., 2014), and memorization learning curves share the exponent (Hutter, 2021; Michaud et al., 2023). Under drift, the n0 = 1 steady state is the freshness objective of crawling (Azar et al., 2018) and the hit law of TTL and random-replacement caches (Choungmo Fofack et al., 2014; Gallo et al., 2014). Square-root refresh and replication rules are classical (Yates et al., 2017; Cohen and Shenker, 2002; Kolobov et al., 2019), and so is abandon-ing fast-changing items (Cho and Garcia-Molina, 2003; Bastopcu and Ulukus, 2021). Square-root allocations also appear in label-efficient inference (Xu et al., 2024; Zrnic and Candès, 2024). The law of practice has been explained by chunk recurrence and by averaging over items (Newell and Rosen-bloom, 1981; Logan, 1988; Heathcote et al., 2000). 

We couple sequential-test sample complexity with occupancy. This gives the fundamental limit of safe one-step coverage under drift, M ⋆ ∼ κsΘ1−1/s ,with a first-order matching converse for every δ-safe policy. 

Safe self-improvement and multi-agent mem-ory. Self-modifying agents accept a change when a benchmark score rises (Zhang et al., 2026a). Per-decision betting gates (Shawn, 2026) and trajectory-level error spending (Wu et al., 2025) control false commits. Earlier antecedents are high-confidence and Seldonian improvement (Thomas et al., 2015, 2019; Chandak et al., 2020) and the “bio-creep” study of approval policies (Feng et al., 2021). Re-cent work proves limits for classifier-style gates (Scrivens, 2026) and near the reward ceiling (Cai et al., 2026). Shared multi-agent memories admit items through judges, one-shot A/B replay or count-and-mean rules (Zhang et al., 2025a; Xu et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2026a). Yet LLM errors are corre-lated (Kim et al., 2025), judges are noisy (Zhang et al., 2026d), and shared memory is a poisoning channel (Dong et al., 2025). These gates decide one modification or one shared item at a time. We study a single memory whose items are certified per template from verifier feedback, and leave shared and self-modifying memories to future work. 

Speed-up learning and the utility problem. 

Symbolic AI compiled multi-step reasoning into macro-operators (Fikes et al., 1972; Korf, 1985), explanation-based rules (Mitchell et al., 1986), chunks (Laird et al., 1986) and compiled produc-tions (Anderson et al., 2004; Taatgen and Lee, 2003). Program synthesis grows libraries in the same way (Ellis et al., 2021). The utility prob-lem (Minton, 1990) led to statistically gated adop-tion (Gratch and DeJong, 1992; Greiner, 1996) and PAC analyses of speed-up learning (Tadepalli and Natarajan, 1996). These analyses assume a fixed problem distribution and never un-adopt a rule. We add four things: validity at all times under adaptive schedules and drift, an indifference zone derived from the loss, retirement with optimal detection de-lay, and closed-form population laws for how much reasoning can be contracted.
