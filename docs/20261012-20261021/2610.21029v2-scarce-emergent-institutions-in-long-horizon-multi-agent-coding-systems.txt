## ABSTRACT 

As autonomous agents become widely deployed, coordinating access to shared computing resources and services will become increasingly important. Recent in-cidents have already revealed unplanned coordination between agents, highlight-ing the safety implications of these interactions. Yet prior studies of agent negoti-ation often use stylized games and simulated bargaining, leaving open how these behaviors develop during sustained, long-horizon tasks. We propose SCARCE 

(Shared Compute AR ena for Coding ag Ents), a framework in which agents solve sequences of coding tasks under individual deadlines while negotiating access to a shared execution resource. This negotiation determines when and for how long they can inspect programs, compile code, and run tests. We find that agents spontaneously develop informal institutions : persistent rules and commitments grounded in reputation, reciprocity, and precedent. These arrangements, which we call institutional behaviors , carry across tasks and influence resource allo-cation, with agents sometimes forgoing immediate advantages to honor earlier agreements. We develop a 21-category taxonomy of negotiation strategies and emergent norms and use it to analyze eight models and audit more than 1,500 tra-jectories across different environmental settings. Among the models tested, newer frontier models exhibit more institutional behaviors than older or lightweight mod-els. Alongside cooperation, we observe exclusionary coalitions and rare divide-and-conquer strategies. SCARCE provides a controlled setting for proactively and systematically auditing how these relationships emerge, shape access to re-sources, and affect the safety and coordination of multi-agent systems.

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Coding agents increasingly run side by side on shared infrastructure, from teams of agents building a compiler in one repository (Carlini, 2026) to hundreds of agents working on a single codebase for weeks (Cursor, 2026). Sharing infrastructure means contending for it. Agents often handle this poorly: they hold locks for too long (Cursor, 2026) or kill another workload to free a resource (Xie et al., 2026). Agents also build social arrangements around what they share. In a recent incident at OpenAI (METR), about 1,200 agents that were meant to be isolated turned a shared package cache into a message board. There, they invented conventions such as HOLD and VETO to manage shared artifacts and pressed peers to follow through on earlier commitments, a phenomenon we call: 

Emergent institution (following North (1990)): a rule, norm, or commitment that agents devise themselves, rather than receive from the environment, and that persists beyond the round in which it was formed to shape later resource allocation. Institutional behavior is any act of discovering, creating, citing, or honoring such a rule. Accounts reconstructed after an incident cannot tell us when such arrangements form, what sustains them, or how they shape access to resources. That requires controlled settings in which agents

Prior work on LLM negotiation and cooperation instead relies on bargaining benchmarks (Bianchi et al., 2024; Davidson et al., 2024; Abdelnabi et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2026b), games such as Diplomacy and Werewolf (O’Neill et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2024; Akata et al., 2025), and simulated societies and markets (Piatti et al., 2024), where the interaction is itself the task and payoffs are set by the game. Multi-agent coding, in turn, has been studied mainly as cooperation toward a shared goal (Qian et al., 2024; Hong et al., 2024), where negotiation appears only rarely (Khatua et al., 2026). To our knowledge, no prior work studies coding agents with independent objectives that repeatedly negotiate over a scarce resource and carry what they learn into later interactions. We introduce SCARCE (Shared Compute AR ena for Coding ag Ents), in which coding agents solve sequences of programming tasks under private deadlines and budgets and must negotiate for time on a single shared execution resource (Figure 1). Tasks come from ProgramBench (Yang et al., 2026), where an agent must rebuild a program from only its executable and documentation. These tasks are long-horizon and far from saturated: the best model on the public leaderboard fully solves 4.5% of them (ProgramBench, 2026). Since agents cannot decompile the binary, running it is the only way to learn what it does, so execution time is a real bottleneck (Section 3). SCARCE lets us vary the number of agents, their budgets, deadlines, and memory, and the number of consecutive tasks (subruns), which further extends the horizon of the interaction. Using a 21-category taxonomy of negotiation strategies and emergent norms that we derive from observed trajectories, and grounded in literature, we analyze more than 1,500 trajectories from 8 models. Agents develop what we call emergent institutions , in the sense of North (1990): persistent rules and commitments, grounded in reciprocity, reputation, and precedent, that shape later alloca-tions. Agents depict Institutional behaviors when they create, cite, and honor such rules. We found agents sometimes give up an immediate advantage to keep an earlier deal; as one put it, “a commit-ment that only holds when it’s free isn’t a commitment.” These institutions require memory across subruns and appear mainly in recent frontier models, whereas older and efficiency-tier models rely on conventions such as shortest-job-first. They are not always benign: we also observe exclusion-ary coalitions and rare divide-and-conquer tactics. Initial advantages, by contrast, do not always entrench, as an agent with a larger starting budget wins early but not late (Section 5). Our contributions are (i) SCARCE , an environment for studying multi-agent coordination and safety in long-horizon coding; (ii) a taxonomy of the negotiation strategies and norms that emerge in it; and (iii) an analysis showing that coding agents build institutions around a scarce resource while solving their tasks to decide which agents get access. As the Hugging Face incident shows (METR), 2Preprint coordination among agents can also serve ends that no operator intended. As labs and developers run growing numbers of agents on shared infrastructure (Salem et al., 2026), understanding how such arrangements form is becoming a prerequisite for deploying these systems safely. 

## 2 BACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK 

Deployment of multi-agent and long-horizon systems. Recent work has increasingly studied LLM-based multi-agent systems in which agents communicate, specialize, and coordinate to solve com-plex programming tasks (Wu et al., 2023; Hong et al., 2023; Zhu et al., 2025). These systems demon-strate sophisticated cooperation and negotiations capabilities. The interactions are often structured around predefined roles or relatively short tasks (Liu et al., 2026a). We study repeatedly interacting coding agents whose independent programming objectives are coupled through competition for a shared execution resource. Long-horizon agents must sustain progress over many steps, decompose goals, and retain relevant information across those subgoals. This has motivated sub-goal based memory work (Hu et al., 2025; Park et al., 2025). Recent work has also extended the study to long-horizon settings like year-long business operations (Fan et al., 2026b) and coding agent coor-dination with shared objectives (Ren et al., 2026; Orimo et al., 2025; Ma et al., 2026). We create an environment to study coding agents that negotiate over a long horizon with independent objectives. 

Benchmarking long-horizon and multi-agent systems. To understand and study long-horizon sys-tems, there have been several benchmarks developed to measure its performance in coding (Deng et al., 2025; Feng et al., 2026; Ding et al., 2025) and other game-like environments (Luo et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026; Piatti et al., 2024). Previous studies have also created benchmarks to study safety (Jiang et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026b). Similar benchmarks exist for multi-agent system performance (Barbarroxa et al., 2025; Chang et al., 2025), safety (Li et al., 2026a; Kavathekar et al., 2026) and privacy (Juneja et al., 2025; Gupta et al., 2026; Gomaa et al., 2026). Our work instead creates a dynamic environment to study negotiation in long-horizon multi-agent coding, focusing on strategies, norms, and institutions that emerge, unprompted, through repeated competition for shared resources. 

Emergent social dynamics. Recent work has explored how persistent agent populations develop collective behaviors and informal governance (Akkil et al., 2026; Paglieri et al., 2026). Paglieri et al. (2026) observe cheating spreading alongside spontaneous auditing and whistleblowing, while other work shows that long-horizon multi-agent systems can also exhibit collusive behavior even in cooperative settings (Nakamura et al., 2026; Shi et al., 2026). We study how similar dynam-ics emerge among coding agents competing for scarce execution resources, including reciprocity, reputation, norms, and coalition formation. 

## 3 THE SCARCE FRAMEWORK 

We adapt ProgramBench (Yang et al., 2026) to build a long-horizon multi-agent coding negotiation framework. ProgramBench is a reconstruction benchmark in which an agent is given a compiled ex-ecutable and its documentation and must reproduce the source code from scratch without accessing the original repository. We choose ProgramBench over simpler benchmarks, due to concerns about benchmark saturation and training-set contamination. ProgramBench, at the moment, does not suf-fer from the same saturation problem, with most frontier models achieving low accuracy. Models need execution time to solve the task, so they need to negotiate to get it. This allows us to test agents’ ability to negotiate while simultaneously completing coding tasks, rather than exploiting benchmark-specific shortcuts, reward hacking, or recalling memorized solutions to achieve the task objective without negotiating for resources. During initial development, we used SWE-Bench (Jimenez et al., 2024), and observed several examples of reward hacking (shown in Appendix B). 3.1 ENVIRONMENT CONSTRUCTION AND CONFIGURATION

To finish each task, agents go through multiple rounds of negotiation. An overview of the system is shown in Figure 2. The framework can be easily configured. It allows modifying the number of participating agents, the prompt for each agent, the deadline for each subrun, the difficulty of the task (by sampling different tasks from ProgramBench), and the budget allocated to each agent. Our results in Section 5 and Appendix C show that these factors affect agent behavior and that this behavior is model-specific, giving developers a tool to audit and study the behavior of their own models. The framework also lets the user choose which components of the negotiation count toward the deadline. For example, they can include negotiation time in the deadline to increase pressure on the agent, or exclude wait time to reduce it. In our experiments, negotiation time does not count toward the agents’ project deadlines, because including it sometimes causes agents to perceive negotiation as a “negotiation tax” and avoid participating in it. As a result, they may rely on the scheduler’s default allocation, which can lead to inefficient scheduling or resource starvation when the sched-uler lacks enough information to make a good allocation. Even though negotiation does not count towards the deadline, waiting in the execution queue does, to increase the deadline pressure. During negotiation, the agents have a hard grant ceiling, i.e., they cannot negotiate and request time higher than a value set before the run starts. This is primarily to increase the number of negotiation rounds per run and to stop an agent from requesting one big slot and monopolizing the run. Once an agent reaches its deadline, the evaluator returns its task performance on the task back to the agent, which uses it to generate a report, containing details about their progress, negotiation strategies utilized, negotiation history, system metadata, and other information that can be useful before starting the next task. In the report, the agent also reflects on the mistakes it made and potential steps it can take to improve upon it. No agent can move on to their next task before all agents have finished the task they started at the same time step. Similar to ProgramBench, each task runs in an independent Docker container, and a task assigned in a given subrun do not overlap across agents or consecutive subruns. 3.2 AGENT SCAFFOLDING

The environment is built to cause a resource scarcity problem. A set of autonomous coding agents, each pursuing their own coding task under their own hard deadline, must share a single execution resource that can be used sequentially. Assuming low memorization, progress on the task is impos-sible without inspecting the target binary, compiling candidate source code, and running tests, none of which can be done on privately owned compute. All the workers compete for time on a single shared execution resource, which a Docker task scheduler allocates based on the negotiation that took place between the agents. The negotiation is a time-slot auction between the agents, where they bid using the credits assigned to them at the start of the run. A solver cannot issue commands until it holds an active execution window. Its only available action outside of a window is a single intercepted tool call, request execution slot .3.4 NEGOTIATION PROTOCOL AND SCHEDULING 

During the negotiation phase (as shown in Appendix D), each agent goes through up to four phases. 

(i) Declaration , where each candidate generates three structured fields, namely desired position, desired duration, and bid, in JSON format based on the resources requested by the solver. (ii) Declaration reasoning , where each agent provides their reasoning, justifying where it believes it should rank and how long its slot should be. (iii) Optional private conversation , where each agent can optionally select a recipient to send a private message to, allowing them to provide and receive additional information and reach consensus. The recipients receive a direct message they can choose not to reply to. If the recipient replies to the direct message, the conversation can last up to R rounds, a variable that is set at the start of the run. After the negotiation ends, each agent casts a ballot ranking the order, the time allocated to each agent, and the bid they are paying. A workers stated bid for the current round is public, but the remaining budget or remaining deadline are strictly private information, as agents are instructed to not disclose this information. 

## 4 TAXONOMY OF NEGOTIATION TACTICS AND INSTITUTIONS 

To characterize how competing coding agents negotiate for shared execution resources, we develop a non-exhaustive taxonomy of 21 categories of strategic and normative behavior classifying the auc-tion trajectories. We construct the taxonomy by providing an LLM with the trajectories and relevant economics and game-theory literature and prompting it to classify the communication, which yields over 30 initial categories. We then manually review all categories and traces, removing those that are redundant or lack sufficient evidence. We then manually cluster similar categories into several different clusters. The definitions of the concepts used here are provided in Table 6 in Appendix A. The taxonomy is organized into two families that we found to be empirically and conceptually dis-tinct. The following group comprises 13 categories derived from classical negotiation theory such as bar-gaining, negotiation theory, repeated games, and reputation theory, etc. We can further divide them into four thematic clusters. 

Reference point and framing. The negotiation can be categorized based on how it justifies the position it is in. (i) Price primary (Vickrey, 1961): By design, to keep the setup realistic, we prompt the agents to use the bids as the primary metric. This way we know that the institutional emergence is not through underspecification, and bidding-based allocation still remains the prompted primary goal. (ii) Anchoring (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974): Agent, instead of modifying or basing the position based on current ask or conversation, maintains its position or bid based on the information that it received early on, in the prior rounds or initial declarations. (iii) Cheap talk (Crawford & Sobel, 1982): Agents use unverifiable signaling to establish credibility with the receiver. For example, an agent losing a bid may describe the inevitable placement as a “concession” forced by external factors, a claim that costs nothing to make and cannot easily be verified. 

Concession and exchange. Agents use private channels to negotiate trades and concessions. (i) Reciprocity (Mailath & Samuelson, 2015): A concession can be contingent on a future action rather than current merit (e.g., “I can offer to raise my bid in a future round as a thank-you for the flexibility” ). (ii) Logrolling (Druckman & Wagner, 2021a): Agents trade information, rather than only future commitments, in exchange for a preferred slot (e.g., “If you are willing to take position 3 instead, I can share the results of my binary help output” ). (iii) Welfare concession (Fehr & Schmidt, 1999): An agent may accept a worse position than its bid would justify to improve collec-tive welfare, such as yielding to a rival nearing its deadline. (iv) Renegotiation (Lee, 2008): When unexpected information arises after an order is settled, agents reopen negotiation to revise the allo-cation, for example when agreed slot conditions prove infeasible or execution behaves unexpectedly. 

Coalitions formation. (i) Explicit coalitions (Ray & Vohra, 1999): What separates coalitions from simple collaboration is in-language acknowledgment of forward-scoped arrangement (e.g., “lets create a standing alternation for the rest of this subrun” ). (ii) Coalition loyalty (Kreps & Wilson, 1982): Once the coalition is formed, the agent can actively forgo a concretely better option to honor the standing coalition arrangement and maintain its loyalty to the coalition. (iii) Exclusionary coalition (Ray & Vohra, 1999): Coalitions lead situations where the agent grants the slot to the favored agent even when the agents contesting the slot have similar requests and conditions.

Our system deliberately provides negotiation mechanism and provides primary metric (bids) but does not provide specific algorithms so that the models can discover and optimize their own algo-rithms. Here we categorize the several norms agents can converge to during the negotiation, which they use to take actions or cite as precedence. 

Temporal and urgency norms. Since the agents’ bids are for time in the cluster, a request that is driven by the deadline, urgency claims become a crucial point of negotiation. (i) Earliest deadline first (Liu & Layland, 1973): One of the simplest orders that the agents can request, especially to resolve tie-breakers, is to order based on deadline. (ii) Sunk-cost urgency (Arkes & Blumer, 1985): is a degenerate version of deadline appeal, where the agent irrationally claims it should go first because its deadline has passed and, hence, it cannot afford to be delayed any further, even though, regardless of the order, the agent will be terminated after the deadline. 

Classical scheduling norms. Agents utilize conventional scheduling techniques for resolving con-flicts, similar to what has been explored in literature (Kleinrock, 1964). (i) Recency first: They can rely on the order in which they entered the negotiation queue, creating a FIFO system. (ii) Shortest job first: Agents classify read-only work as the shorter work that can go first, whereas more com-plex implementation workloads as something that takes less priority. (iii) Readiness/variance first: 

Agents over multiple runs improve their understanding of the task and use it to predict the likelihood of a task failing, which they can use to propose to push the task that can potentially fail later. 

Process norms and negotiation governing. The negotiation or bargaining strategies display some basic norms. (i) Iterative bargaining (Rubinstein, 1982): The agents norms necessarily do not arise from simple one message agreement. They are also an outcome of multi-round bargain-ing, where each agent adjusts its stance before reaching an agreement. (ii) In-negotiation rule-making (Young, 1993): Mid-negotiation, agents can use the information so far to propose rules that are utilized moving forward so they are just cited instead of renegotiated. (iii) Scope reduc-tion (Druckman & Wagner, 2021b): Agents can declare a certain issue not worth negotiating on, leading to focus on a smaller topic or terminating the conversation altogether. 4.3 INSTITUTIONAL BEHAVIOR

By the definition of an institution, norms are created to influence agents’ behavior across multiple rounds. These institutional behaviors can be clustered into two subgroups: 

Relational behavior. These institutional behaviors are linked through persistent relationships, driven by commitment, reputation, or agent-specific precedent. Under this category, we include reciprocity, logrolling, reputation, coalition formation, loyalty, and coalition-based exclusion. These relations promote temporary cooperation in this negotiation setting. 

Non-relational behavior. Agents can also develop behaviors that do not target individual relation-ships but instead apply as persistent norms across all members or a large subset of them. These include social proof, welfare concession, and rule-making. 

## 5 RESULTS 

Our experiments aim to answer three main questions: (1) What are the primary strategies used by coding agents during negotiation, and how do they differ across models, (2) what are the institutional strategies that the models can utilize over multiple rounds of conversations, and (3) how do different environment states affect the strategies used by the agent, i.e., what is the impact of an agent having higher budget or more time compared to other agents. We hypothesize that while the model itself determines whether institutional behavior emerges, the environment controls the degree and type of behavior displayed. That is, if a model is capable of institutional behavior, then variation in budgets, deadlines, number of steps, and similar parameters will change which behaviors and which agents dominate. For example, agents with a larger budget than others can use it to dominate in the current round or preserve it for later rounds. Agents with the nearest deadline may either become more aggressive or stop competing altogether because they estimate they cannot finish the task anyway. To do so, we first classify the behavior seen in our trajectories using the categories defined in our taxonomy, and then we perform ablations by changing variables like the agents’ budget, deadlines, and memory availability (of previous subruns) to demonstrate and analyze the difference in emergent institutional behavior. 8Preprint 5.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Environment settings. We use five agents across all experiments, with a starting budget of 1,000 and a deadline of 25 minutes in the base setting. Each test runs for up to four subruns. In the ablation settings, we vary the deadline from 10 to 25 minutes and the budget from 1,000 to 1,500. We also compare agent performance depending on whether agents can modify their bid mid-run. 

Models. We conduct our experiments using large frontier models such as Opus 5 (Anthropic, a), Kimi-k3 (Bai et al., 2026), Kimi K2.6 (Team, 2026), GLM-5.3 (Zai, b), GLM-5.2 (Zai, a), and smaller efficiency models such as GPT-5.6 Luna (OpenAI, 2026), Haiku-4.5 (Anthropic, b), and gpt-oss-120b (Agarwal et al., 2025) for our large-scale testing. However, most of our ablations use GLM-5.3 and GPT-5.6 Luna as a cost-saving measure. 

Metrics. We use an LLM-as-judge to classify trajectories into the categories defined in our tax-onomy. Specifically, the percentage of rounds that show a given behavior or strategy. 5.4 IMPACT OF DIFFERENT STARTING STATES 

In practice, agents may not all start under the same conditions. For example, agents may work asynchronously, or join the negotiation later than others. We therefore vary the starting states of agents to study how an agent’s state impacts the strategy it uses. We vary two factors. First, we study the impact of an uneven budget, where one agent starts with 500 more credits than the others. Does the budget difference affect how aggressive the agents are? Does the agent with the higher budget dominate during the entire run? Second, we study the impact of uneven time, where each agent is assigned a different deadline ranging from 10 to 25 minutes per task. Does having more time than other agents make an agent more charitable? Does an agent with a closer deadline become more aggressive? In Table 5, we observe that agents that start with a higher budget do not dominate across the entire run. They spend their budget in the early rounds, achieving a high win rate, and then drop to a win rate below the uniform baseline by the final rounds. In Table 4, we observe that agents with less time, and therefore closer to their deadlines, bid more aggressively in early subruns to secure more time through an early execution slot, we call this paying an urgency premium . However, in later subruns, agents with urgent deadlines bid less and request less time than agents with more time, thereby disengaging from price competition rather than escalating it. We name this behavior termination discount . Based on the traces shown in Figure 9, we hypothesize that this occurs because the agents develop better reasoning about task feasibility. As a result, they step aside when resources are scarce, recognizing that the remaining time is insufficient to finish the task. 

## 6 DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION 

SCARCE shows that when coding agents repeatedly share a scarce resource, the mechanism an operator specifies is not always the mechanism that may end up allocating the resource. Agents were instructed to treat bids as the primary signal. Even so, recent frontier models built their own persistent rules on top of the auction, including tie-breaks, reputation, standing alternations, and reciprocity debts. These arrangements were negotiated largely through private channels and sus-11 Preprint tained by memory across subruns (Figure 6 and Table 2). This has three implications for deploying multi-agent systems. First, the guarantees of a well-designed allocation mechanism may not survive once agents can communicate privately and maintain memory, so these arrangements need to be audited continually. Second, the line between coordination and collusion is thin. A standing al-ternation between two bidders is analogous to bid rotation, and exclusionary coalitions shift access from some principals to others without anyone authorizing it (Calvano et al., 2020; Hammond et al., 2025). Third, agents make commitments their principals never sanctioned. An agent may give up a better slot to honor a deal with a peer, or stop competing for time once it judges its own task in-feasible (Figure 9 and Table 4). This behavior appears in recent frontier models, and is absent from efficiency-tier models where it is not induced by additional reasoning effort (Table 9). It is there-fore likely to become more common and should be measured before deployment. We do not claim that all of these behaviors necessarily imply misalignment; however, they may result in unexpected behaviors. 

Limitations and future work. Several questions remain open. We have not established whether these institutions improve or degrade collective outcomes. Answering this requires relating institu-tional behavior to deadline misses, task progress, and the distribution of execution time, and compar-ing against a centralized scheduler. The institutions are also fragile. Relational arrangements erode into bidding wars late in a run, and the advantage of a larger budget has only early advantages for some models but durable for others (Table 5 and Table 7). Part of this erosion may come from de-sign choices: after-subrun reports encourage agents to discard any strategy that did not complete the task. Disentangling these effects, and testing whether agents develop enforcement mechanisms such as sanctioning defectors, is an important next step. Finally, real deployments will involve mixed populations of models, many more agents, and newcomers joining established agentic systems. If frontier agents form coalitions that efficiency-tier agents cannot join, users of cheaper agents may be systematically disadvantaged. Rare behaviors such as divide-and-conquer or meta-gaming, which we observe only occasionally, may become more frequent at scale. Because these institutions are shaped by memory, memory design, including what is retained and what is compacted, should be evaluated together with the model as part of governing multi-agent systems.

Table 7 reports how GPT-5.6-luna’s advantaged (“rich”) worker behaves as its per-subrun budget is drawn down under the unequal-budget setting. C.5 SUBRUN AND ROUND LEVEL BEHAVIORAL ANALYSIS

We hypothesize that this primarily occurs because agent capable of updating their bids realize that consistent renegotiation is costly and has little gains and they attempt to reduce cost, which is possible with a higher reliance on pre-established rules.
